MATH FIRST - HOW TO ATTACK ANY CALCULATION. (1) Underline what is asked and the units (per 100, per 1,000, per 100,000, percent, "to the hundredth"). (2) Write the numbers into the right table or formula before calculating. (3) Keep full calculator precision and round ONLY the final answer, unless the test prints rounded intermediate values. (4) Write the formula, the substitution, the answer WITH units, and one sentence of interpretation in plain words (partial credit is common). (5) Sanity check: a proportion is 0-1 (or 0-100%), RR and OR are never negative, sensitivity/specificity are 0-100%. Typical directions: "round to the nearest hundredth" for RR/OR/ratios, "tenth of a percent" for attack rates and percentages, "per 1,000" or "per 100,000" for population rates, 2-3 significant figures for statistics. Tests often hand you extra distractor numbers (hospitalized counts, total population, number of foods) - use only the numbers the definition needs.

THE UNIVERSAL 2x2 TABLE (draw it every time). Rows = exposure (ate the food / attended / vaccinated / smoker / test positive). Columns = outcome (ill vs well / case vs control / disease vs no disease). Letters a, b, c, d are as in the diagram (a = exposed and ill, d = unexposed and well; N = a+b+c+d). DANGER: tests often print the table turned sideways (ill on top, "Case/Control" by "Attended/Did not"), put the unexposed row first, or label columns "Has disease"; RELABEL a, b, c, d yourself before using any formula. Check that a+b+c+d equals the number of people stated (some tests print totals that do not add up - then just compute from the cells as written). When only the exposed row is given (typical of "which food made people sick"), you can compute the attack rate among eaters but not RR.

1. ATTACK RATE (AR) = risk = cumulative incidence in a group followed over a short outbreak. Always divide the number ill by the people IN THAT GROUP (x 100 for percent), never by the total number ill. Overall AR = all ill / N. FOOD-SPECIFIC attack rate: for each food, AR among people who ate it vs AR among people who did not eat it. HOW TO FIND THE GUILTY FOOD: make the 2x2 for every food; the culprit has (i) a HIGH attack rate among eaters, (ii) a LOW attack rate among non-eaters, so the biggest difference or biggest ratio, and (iii) almost every ill person ate it (a is nearly all of a+c). If every food has RR close to 1.0 (all between about 0.8 and 1.2), no food is implicated - suspect person-to-person spread or a non-food source. Secondary attack rate (SAR) = new cases among susceptible contacts of an index case / number of susceptible contacts x 100; the index/primary cases are NOT in the denominator (6 sick children go home, 4 of their 10 siblings catch it: SAR = 4/10 = 40%). Example: potluck, ate potato salad: 40 ill, 20 well; did not eat it: 10 ill, 40 well. AR eaters = 40/60 = 66.7%; AR non-eaters = 10/50 = 20.0%. Another: ate salad 30 ill of 40 eaters = 75.0%; non-eaters 10 ill of 60 = 16.7%; ate pie 25 of 50 = 50% vs 15 of 50 = 30%: salad is the culprit (bigger gap). If the table lists foods only for eaters (Spaghetti 22 ill of 58 = 0.379; Caviar 52 of 86 = 0.605; Steak 12 of 52 = 0.231) the highest eater AR (caviar) is named the culprit and the measure asked is "attack rate".

2. RELATIVE RISK (RR) = risk ratio = attack-rate ratio. Use for COHORT designs (known groups, e.g. everyone at a wedding, everyone who got the vaccine or not, clinical trials, retrospective cohorts of a defined guest list) because you know the size of both groups. Interpretation: RR = 1 no association; RR > 1 exposure is a risk factor ("exposed people were RR times as likely to get ill"); RR < 1 exposure is PROTECTIVE (never say "negative risk"). Same potluck example: RR = 66.7% / 20.0% = 3.33 (people who ate it were 3.33 times as likely to become ill). Another: 36/120 = 0.30 vs 9/90 = 0.10, RR = 3.0. If AR in exposed is 43% and unexposed 54%, RR = 0.79: the "suspect" water looks protective - do not force a cause. Say "2.5 times as likely" NOT "2.5% higher". RR cannot be computed from a case-control study. Risk difference = attributable risk = AR exposed - AR unexposed (extra cases per 100 exposed; potluck 66.7 - 20.0 = 46.7 percentage points). Attributable fraction among the exposed (AF% or AR%) = (AR exposed - AR unexposed)/AR exposed = (RR-1)/RR x 100 (potluck: (3.33-1)/3.33 = 70.0%: 70% of the exposed's illness is due to the exposure). Population attributable risk = AR in whole population - AR in unexposed; PAF% = that / AR in whole population.

3. ODDS RATIO (OR). The OR is the odds of exposure in cases divided by the odds of exposure in controls (formula in the diagram). Use for CASE-CONTROL studies (people picked because they have the disease, plus comparison people without it) - there the group sizes are chosen by the investigator, so risks and RR are meaningless; the OR is the only valid measure. Case-control layout: columns = cases | controls (a = exposed cases, d = unexposed controls). Steps: (1) label cells, (2) a x d, (3) b x c, (4) divide. Example (potluck case-control): cases attended 65, did not attend 2; controls attended 43, did not 18: OR = (65 x 18)/(2 x 43) = 1170/86 = 13.60 - the odds of attending were 13.6 times higher in cases than in controls (strong association). The same potluck data would give a wrong "RR" of 6.0. Potluck cohort table above: OR = (40 x 40)/(20 x 10) = 8.00 (RR was 3.33: OR is farther from 1 than RR when the outcome is common). Interpretation words: "the odds of exposure were OR times higher among cases"; for a CASE-CONTROL OR say odds, not "times more likely to get the disease" unless the disease is rare. Rare-disease assumption: when disease is rare (about under 10%) a and c are tiny so OR approximates RR. OR < 1 protective; OR = 1 none. If any cell is 0, OR is undefined/infinite (no control was exposed). Matched-pair case-control (each case matched to one control): build a paired table and use ONLY the discordant pairs, b/c (b = case exposed but control not; e.g. 14 and 11 give OR = 1.27; 27 and 12 give 2.25). Do NOT use ad/bc on matched data. McNemar chi-square = (b - c)^2/(b + c), df 1 (e.g. (14-11)^2/25 = 0.36, not significant). Mantel-Haenszel (stratified) OR = sum over strata of (a_i d_i / n_i) divided by sum of (b_i c_i / n_i); it adjusts for a confounder (age group) by making one 2x2 per stratum and pooling; if stratum ORs differ a lot, that is effect modification. Odds from a probability: odds = p/(1-p); p = odds/(1+odds). Prevalence ratio (cross-sectional) = prevalence in exposed / prevalence in unexposed. Rate ratio/rate difference use person-time rates the same way.

WHICH MEASURE? (extras to the flowchart). Ecological: no individual measures. Only exposed eaters listed: attack rate (highest eater AR = culprit). Test result vs true disease: sensitivity/specificity. Counts over time in a population: incidence/prevalence. If the question says "state the measure and why", name it AND justify it with the design.

4. SENSITIVITY, SPECIFICITY, PPV, NPV (screening and diagnostic tests). Build the table with TRUE disease status (from the gold standard) in COLUMNS and TEST result in ROWS. Some tests flip it (test in columns) - check the labels, because the denominators flip with it. Sensitivity and specificity divide by a COLUMN total (sensitivity = the SICK column: of the truly sick, the percent the test catches; specificity = the HEALTHY column: of the truly healthy, the percent the test clears). PPV and NPV divide by a ROW total (PPV = the test+ row: of all positive tests, the percent truly sick; NPV = the test- row: of all negative tests, the percent truly healthy). Accuracy = (TP+TN)/total. Prevalence = (TP+FN)/total. False-positive rate = FP/(FP+TN) = 1 - specificity (specificity is NOT the false-positive rate). False-negative rate = 1 - sensitivity. LR+ = sensitivity/(1 - specificity); LR- = (1 - sensitivity)/specificity. Memory: SnNout (high Sensitivity, Negative result rules OUT) and SpPin (high Specificity, Positive rules IN). Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test and do NOT change with prevalence; PPV and NPV DO change: higher prevalence raises PPV and lowers NPV; rare disease gives low PPV even for a good test. Example: TP 135, FP 90, FN 16, TN 532 (N = 773): sensitivity 135/151 = 89.40%; specificity 532/622 = 85.53%; PPV 135/225 = 60.00%; NPV 532/548 = 97.08%; accuracy 667/773 = 86.29%. When only sensitivity, specificity, prevalence and N are given: (1) sick = N x prevalence; healthy = N - sick; (2) TP = sens x sick, FN = sick - TP; (3) TN = spec x healthy, FP = healthy - TN; (4) compute PPV, NPV. Example: sens 95%, spec 95%, prevalence 1%, N = 10,000: sick 100, TP 95, FN 5; healthy 9,900, TN 9,405, FP 495; PPV = 95/(95+495) = 16.1%, NPV = 9,405/9,410 = 99.95% (a positive result is wrong 5 times in 6 because the disease is rare). Bayes form: PPV = sens x prev / [sens x prev + (1-spec)(1-prev)]. Choosing: screening/outbreak (do not miss cases) wants HIGH SENSITIVITY (and NPV), then confirm positives with a specific test; confirming a diagnosis before drastic treatment wants HIGH SPECIFICITY. A broad (sensitive) case definition early in an outbreak; narrow (specific) later. Two tests: in SERIES (both must be positive) specificity and PPV go UP, sensitivity DOWN; in PARALLEL (either positive) sensitivity and NPV go UP, specificity DOWN. Back-solving a missing cell: use the formula that includes it (FP = TP x (1-PPV)/PPV; FN = TP x (1-sens)/sens; TP = PPV x number positive).

5. INCIDENCE, PREVALENCE AND RATES (plain steps). Incidence = how many NEW cases appeared; prevalence = how many people HAVE the disease (old + new). Incidence proportion (risk) = new cases in the period divided by the people who were disease-free at the start (the people "at risk"), times 10^n. Incidence rate = new cases divided by total person-time (add up each person's years while still disease-free), e.g. per 1,000 person-years. Point prevalence = cases on one date; period prevalence = anyone who had the disease at ANY time in the period (both divided by the population). Prevalence = incidence x average duration, with both in the same time unit (incidence 1.65 per 100,000 per YEAR, illness lasts 18 months = 1.5 YEARS: 1.65 x 1.5 = 2.475 per 100,000). High incidence with low prevalence = short or quickly fatal illness; low incidence with high prevalence = long-lasting illness; prevalence rises when people live longer with the disease or the cure rate falls. A "per N" rate: cases / population, then multiply by N (city of 200,000 with 500 new cases in a year: 500/200,000 = 0.0025 x 100,000 = 250 per 100,000 per year). Prevalence 1,200 / 200,000 = 0.006 = 6 per 1,000. Turning a rate back into a count: 101.9 per 100,000 x 39,500,000 people = 101.9 x 395 = 40,250.5 cases. Percent change = (new - old)/old x 100 ((150 - 68)/68 = 120.6% increase). Person-time: a person who becomes a case or leaves the study stops counting (count half a year for that year ONLY if the test says so). Example: 7 new cases over 3,192.5 person-years = 0.002193 = 2.19 per 1,000 person-years. Proportion = the top is part of the bottom (attack rate, CFR); ratio = one group over a separate group (male:female); rate = time is in the bottom. Unit conversion: 0.00025 = 25 per 100,000 = 0.25 per 1,000. Diagram questions (lines for patients over a month): incidence = lines that START inside the period; point prevalence = lines crossing one date; period prevalence = every line touching the period. A rise in reported cases with no real change can come from better reporting, a new test, publicity, a changed case definition, a bigger denominator, lab error or a newly notifiable disease.

6. MORTALITY AND OTHER RATES (what goes on top and bottom). Case fatality rate (CFR) = deaths FROM the disease / people WITH the disease x 100 (10 deaths among 68 cases = 14.7%). Mortality (death) rate = deaths / the WHOLE population (per 1,000 or 100,000); crude death rate = all deaths / midyear population; cause-specific mortality = deaths from one cause / whole population; age-specific (or sex-specific) rate = deaths in that group / people in the SAME group (340 / 20,000 = 17 per 1,000); proportionate mortality = deaths from the cause / ALL deaths (20 of 200 = 10%); infant mortality = deaths before age 1 / live births x 1,000; neonatal = deaths under 28 days / live births (35/2,600 = 13.46 per 1,000); crude birth rate = births / midyear population x 1,000; fertility rate = live births / women aged 15-44 x 1,000; death-to-case ratio = deaths / cases. Germ traits: infectivity = infected / exposed; pathogenicity = got clinically sick / infected; virulence = severe or died / got clinically sick (a germ that infects many, sickens few and rarely kills = infectivity HIGH, pathogenicity LOW, virulence LOW). AGE-ADJUSTED RATES let you compare two populations with different age mixes. DIRECT (study rates applied to a STANDARD population; table in the diagram): rates 60, 300, 750 per 100,000 in groups whose standard populations are 600k, 300k, 100k give expected 360 + 900 + 750 = 2,010 per 1,000,000 = 201.0 per 100,000 (the crude rate could be very different). INDIRECT (standard rates x the study population give EXPECTED deaths; see the table): SMR = 270/285 = 0.95; SMR 149 = 49% more deaths than expected; SMR 1155 = about 12 times as many. Use direct when your age-specific rates are reliable; use indirect for small numbers (workplace groups). If two crude rates are equal but the age-adjusted rate is higher in A, A has the YOUNGER population. Years of potential life lost (YPLL): for each death before the chosen age, life expectancy minus age at death, added up (life expectancy 78, deaths at 30, 60, 85 = 48 + 18 + 0 = 66; a death past the cutoff adds 0). DALY = YLL + YLD: years of life lost to early death plus years lived with disability (Disability-ADJUSTED Life Years, NOT "daily"). Doubling time = 70 / growth rate in percent (2% a year = 35 years). Population pyramid: wide base = high birth rate and young population; modal group = the widest bar; median group = where the running total reaches half.

7. VACCINE / TREATMENT EFFECT (steps). Vaccine efficacy (in a trial) or effectiveness (real world): (1) attack rate in the vaccinated, (2) attack rate in the unvaccinated, (3) divide vaccinated by unvaccinated to get RR, (4) VE = 1 - RR, x 100 for percent. Examples: 30/10,000 = 0.30% vs 80/10,000 = 0.80%: RR 0.375, VE = 62.5%. 5% vs 10%: VE 50%. 2% vs 20%: 90%. Incidence 2 vs 10 per 1,000 person-years: RR 0.2, VE 80%. Same idea as relative risk reduction (RRR) = (control risk - treated risk)/control risk. Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = control risk - treated risk, in percentage points (9.0% - 6.0% = 3.0 points). NNT = 1/ARR = how many people must be treated to prevent one case (1/0.03 = 33.3; always round UP to 34). For harm: risk in the exposed minus risk in the unexposed (3.0% vs 1.0% = 0.020); NNH = 1 / that (1/0.02 = 50). Cost to prevent one case = NNT x cost per patient (33.3 x $1,000 = $33,333; compare with the savings per case). A big RRR can be a tiny ARR when baseline risk is low, so always give the baseline risk. Efficacy = ideal trial conditions; effectiveness = real-world data (US flu VE networks = effectiveness). Efficiency = outputs per resources used.

8. R0, HERD IMMUNITY AND OUTBREAK MODELS (steps). R0 = the average number of people one sick person infects in a fully susceptible population. R0 below 1: the outbreak dies out; R0 = 1: steady (endemic); R0 above 1: it grows. R0 depends on the germ and on how people behave, NOT on how deadly it is. Finding a missing piece of R0 (= contacts x chance of passing it on per contact x days infectious): R0 2.87 with 3 contacts a day and 8 days gives chance = 2.87/(3 x 8) = 0.1196 = 12%. Halving contacts halves R0 (12 to 6). HIT steps: 1 divided by R0, subtract from 1, x 100 (R0 = 5: 1 - 0.2 = 80%; measles R0 15: 93.3%; R0 3: 66.7%; R0 1.5: 33.3%; R0 2.37: 57.81%; R0 24: 95.83%). People to vaccinate = HIT x population, round UP (34,000 people, R0 3.5: 0.7143 x 34,000 = 24,286). If the vaccine is not perfect: coverage needed = HIT / vaccine efficacy (R0 3 gives HIT 66.7%; efficacy 75% needs 0.667/0.75 = 88.9% coverage; an answer over 100% means vaccination alone cannot do it, e.g. 91.7%/0.90 = 101.9%). To test an option, multiply coverage x efficacy; the result must be at least the HIT (0.95 x 0.75 = 0.7125 < 0.75, so it fails). Re (effective reproduction number, R right now) = R0 x the fraction still susceptible (R0 10, 85% susceptible: Re = 8.5; R0 12 with 88% immune: 12 x 0.12 = 1.44); to bring Re down to 1 you need an immune fraction of 1 - 1/R0; the next round of cases = Re x current cases (1.1 x 150 = 165). If two groups differ in contacts, vaccinate the high-contact group first. Rounds of spread: each case infects R others, and one round takes one serial interval. With R = 2 and 1 day per round: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 > 24 classmates, so 4 days. "24 infected reach 8,600 in 6 days": 24 x R^6 >= 8,600 so R >= 2.67, use 3. SIR MODEL: people move from S (can catch it) to I (infected) to R (recovered and immune); the population is constant, well mixed, with no births or deaths (equations in the diagram). Tests may drop the /N: new infections = a x S x I and recoveries = b x I (a = transmission coefficient, b = recovery rate; days infected = 1/b). EULER STEPS: new value = old value + its change for one day (or week). Example a = 0.0005, b = 0.125, S = 9,700, I = 300, R = 0: new infections = 0.0005 x 9,700 x 300 = 1,455; S changes by -1,455; recoveries = 0.125 x 300 = 37.5; I changes by 1,455 - 37.5 = 1,417.5; R changes by +37.5; next day S = 8,245, I = 1,717.5, R = 37.5 (if told "round to integers", round each value as you go and reuse the rounded values); days infected = 1/0.125 = 8. Early in an epidemic S falls, I rises, R rises. Mask mandates lower transmission (beta). SEIR adds an Exposed (latent) stage (sigma = 1/incubation period); SEIRS adds waning immunity (waves). A higher R0 gives a higher AND earlier epidemic peak.

9. CHI-SQUARE TEST OF ASSOCIATION (2x2) - steps. It answers: is the link between exposure and illness bigger than chance alone would give? (1) Write H0 (no link: exposure and illness are independent) and H1 (there is a link). (2) Write the observed 2x2 with row and column totals. (3) For each cell find the EXPECTED count, meaning what you would see if there were no link: row total x column total / grand total. (4) For each of the 4 cells compute (observed - expected)^2 / expected, then ADD the four answers: that sum is the chi-square. (5) Degrees of freedom = 1 for a 2x2 table. (6) Compare with the cutoff: 3.84 (p = 0.05), 6.63 (p = 0.01), 10.83 (p = 0.001); if your chi-square is BIGGER, reject H0 = statistically significant. Example (bear meat; ate: 54 sick, 7 well; did not eat: 14 sick, 32 well; N = 107): sick total 68, well 39, ate 61, did not eat 46. Expected: a = 61x68/107 = 38.77; b = 61x39/107 = 22.23; c = 46x68/107 = 29.23; d = 46x39/107 = 16.77. Every observed-minus-expected comes out as 15.23 (54 - 38.77, 7 - 22.23, ...), so chi-square = 15.23^2/38.77 + 15.23^2/22.23 + 15.23^2/29.23 + 15.23^2/16.77 = 5.98 + 10.43 + 7.94 + 13.83 = 38.2, bigger than 3.84: reject H0 (p < 0.001). Shortcut for a 2x2: chi-square = N x (ad - bc)^2 / [(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)]. The Yates correction lowers the value (use it only if told). Chi-square only says whether a link is real, NOT how strong it is or which direction (a "significant" protective OR means the exposure is NOT the cause). WHICH TEST? Any expected count under 5 (small outbreak): Fisher's exact. Matched pairs: McNemar. 2x2 tables split by a confounder: Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel. One variable's observed vs expected counts: chi-square goodness-of-fit. Compare 2 averages: t test; same people measured twice: paired t test; 3 or more averages: ANOVA; large samples or comparing proportions: z test. Correlation r (Pearson) = strength and direction of a straight-line relationship, from -1 to +1. Cohen's kappa = (agreement seen - agreement expected by chance)/(1 - agreement expected by chance), from 0 to 1.

10. DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS (steps). Mean = add all the values, divide by how many there are. Median = sort low to high and take the middle one; with an even count average the two middle values (10, 14, 18, 28, 34, 46, 54, 60, 72, 86: median (34+46)/2 = 40, mean 42.2). Mode = the most common value (there can be several, or none if all differ). Range = biggest minus smallest. Quartiles (box-plot diagram): Q1 = median of the lower half, Q3 = median of the upper half (2, 10, 17, 20, 37, 40, 50: median 20, Q1 = median of 2, 10, 17 = 10, Q3 = median of 37, 40, 50 = 40; five-number summary 2, 10, 20, 40, 50). One extreme value drags the MEAN but barely moves the median or the IQR (median 1.5 vs mean 20.8 when one value is 360). Which average to report: categories (sex, blood type) = mode; ordered categories, skewed data or outliers = median; ratios, titers or growth by multiplying = geometric mean (multiply all n values, then take the nth root: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 multiply to 32,768, fifth root = 8). STANDARD DEVIATION (SD = how spread out the data are), steps: (1) find the mean; (2) subtract the mean from each value; (3) square each result; (4) add them up; (5) divide by n - 1 for a sample (by n for a whole population; assume sample unless told "population"), which gives the variance; (6) take the square root = SD. Example 13, 15, 4, 4, 1: mean 7.4; squared differences 31.36, 57.76, 11.56, 11.56, 40.96 add to 153.2; variance 153.2/4 = 38.3; SD = 6.19 (population version: 30.64 and 5.54). SD is in the original units; variance is in squared units; range, variance and SD all get pulled by extreme values. z-score = how many SDs a value is from the mean = (value - mean)/SD (91 when mean 82.65, SD 2.40: z = 3.48, very unusual; beyond +/-3 counts as an outlier). Empirical rule (68-95-99.7 in the diagram): mean 60, SD 15: 45 to 75 is within 1 SD = 68%. Probability of exactly k successes in n tries = C(n,k) p^k (1-p)^(n-k); first success on try k = (1-p)^(k-1) x p (p = 0.07, k = 5: 0.93^4 x 0.07 = 0.0524). Variable types: nominal (names), ordinal (ordered), interval (no true zero: temperature, dates), ratio (true zero: weight, counts, time); discrete (counts) vs continuous. Percent of a group = part / whole ((37+76)/204 = 55.4%).

11. CONFIDENCE INTERVALS, t TESTS AND p-VALUES (plain steps). CONFIDENCE INTERVAL (CI) FOR A MEAN = a range that probably contains the true average. Steps: (1) find the mean and the SD. (2) Standard error: SE = SD divided by the square root of the sample size n. (3) Pick the multiplier for the confidence level: 90% = 1.645, 95% = 1.96, 96% = 2.05, 99% = 2.576. (4) Margin = multiplier x SE. (5) CI = mean minus margin, to mean plus margin. Example: mean 90, SD 6, n = 1,000, 96% CI: SE = 6/31.62 = 0.19; margin = 2.05 x 0.19 = 0.39; CI = 89.6 to 90.4. With a small sample (n under 30) use a t multiplier instead, found from n - 1 (about 2.13 for n = 16, 2.26 for n = 10). CI FOR A PERCENT (p written as a decimal): SE = square root of [p x (1 - p) / n]; margin = 1.96 x SE. Example 40 of 100 (p = 0.40): SE = square root of (0.4 x 0.6 / 100) = 0.049; margin = 0.096; CI = 30.4% to 49.6%. t TEST (is the sample average different from a target value?): (1) difference = sample mean - target; (2) SE = SD / square root of n; (3) t = difference / SE; (4) compare the size of t (ignore the minus sign) with the t multiplier for that n (about 2.13 for n = 16); bigger = statistically significant. Example: mean 24.1, SD 5.6, n = 16, target 38.0: SE = 5.6/4 = 1.4; t = (24.1 - 38.0)/1.4 = -9.93; 9.93 is far past 2.13, so significant (the minus sign only means the sample is below the target). PAIRED t TEST (same people measured twice, e.g. before and after): subtract each person's two values, then run the t test above on those differences with a target of 0. Limits of agreement between two measuring methods = average difference +/- 1.96 x SD of the differences. READING A CI FOR AN OR OR RR: 1 means no effect. If the range does NOT include 1 it is significant; if it includes 1 it is NOT. OR 3.5 with CI 1.2-8.8: significant, but the wide range means an imprecise estimate. CI 0.8-4.5 includes 1: not significant. CI 1.26-1.99 means 26% to 99% higher odds. A wider interval = less precise = usually a smaller sample. Correct wording: if the study were repeated many times, 95% of such intervals would contain the true value. p-VALUE = how likely results this extreme would be by pure chance if there were really NO effect (the null hypothesis). Small p (under 0.05) = unlikely to be chance = "statistically significant" = reject the null. It does NOT prove cause, it is not the chance that the null is true, and it gets smaller as the sample gets bigger; p < 0.01 does not mean "99% sure". TWO KINDS OF MISTAKE: Type I error (alpha) = false alarm (you say there is an effect, but there is none); Type II error (beta) = a miss (you say there is no effect, but there is one). Power = 1 - beta = the chance of catching a real effect (80% is enough); more subjects, a bigger real effect, a larger alpha or less scatter all raise power. Random error (luck of the sample) shrinks with a bigger sample; systematic error (bias) does not. Null hypothesis = nothing going on: no difference, no association, no effect (RR or OR = 1).

12. EPI-CURVE AND INCUBATION MATH (steps). Incubation period = time from exposure to first symptoms (onset time minus exposure time; convert hours and days carefully). Party Feb 13 at 7 pm, first onset Feb 14 at 2 pm = 19 hours; last onset Feb 21 at 9 am = 182 hours (7 days 14 h); the incubation RANGE is 19 to 182 hours. Median incubation = time from exposure to the PEAK of a point-source curve. Unknown exposure time: use the exposure-window rule in the diagram (first case Mar 27, last Apr 19, incubation 9-48 days: start = Mar 27 - 48 d = Feb 7, end = Apr 19 - 9 d = Apr 10, a 62-day window; then ask what everyone did in that window). With the peak and a known median incubation: exposure date = peak date - median incubation. Shapes (pictured): POINT SOURCE = one sharp peak within one incubation period (single exposure; wedding, picnic); CONTINUOUS common source = plateau; INTERMITTENT = irregular peaks; PROPAGATED = successive taller waves about one incubation period apart (person to person), ending when susceptible people run out or control starts. Pick x-axis intervals of about 1/4 to 1/3 of the incubation period. Index case = first case that comes to attention; primary = first person infected; secondary = infected by a case. An outbreak is declared over after 2 x the maximum incubation period with no new cases (Ebola 21 d: 42 days); declaring it sooner is too early. Very late or early cases = secondary cases, a different exposure or an unusual incubation.

13. WORDING TRAPS SEEN ON REAL TESTS. (a) "Attack rate" may be asked on case-control data (cases among eaters/all eaters) - compute as written, but the proper measure there is OR. (b) A stem that tells a story ("the water caused it") may have RR < 1 - answer what the numbers show. (c) "Rate" is often loosely used for proportions (cases/residents "per 1,000"). (d) Prevalence "during July" = period prevalence. (e) Table totals that do not add up - use cells. (f) Directions that conflict ("thousandths" vs "significant figures") - give the decimal asked and mention the other. (g) Show formula, substitution, units, interpretation. (h) Two 2x2 tables for 5-10 foods: compute all, compare. (i) PPV questions at 1% prevalence: expect a low answer. (j) A matched or paired design needs the matched formula. (k) HIT uses R0 only (not attack rate or OR). (l) RR and OR describe association, not proof of causation (needs Hill criteria, confounders ruled out).

FULL WORKED OUTBREAK (do this on any "find the culprit" table). A wedding: 100 guests interviewed, 60 ill. Chicken: ate 50 (45 ill, 5 well), did not eat 50 (15 ill, 35 well). Cake: ate 70 (42 ill, 28 well), did not 30 (18 ill, 12 well). Rice: ate 40 (28 ill, 12 well), did not 60 (32 ill, 28 well). STEP 1 design: a known guest list = retrospective cohort, so use attack rates and RR. STEP 2 attack rates (eaters vs non-eaters): chicken 90% vs 30%; cake 60% vs 60%; rice 70% vs 53.3%. STEP 3 RR: chicken 3.00; cake 1.00; rice 1.31. STEP 4 (optional) OR = ad/bc: chicken (45 x 35)/(5 x 15) = 21.0; cake 1.00; rice 2.04. STEP 5 chi-square (shortcut N(ad-bc)^2/[(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)]): chicken 100 x (1575 - 75)^2/(50 x 50 x 60 x 40) = 37.5 > 3.84, significant; cake 0; rice 2.78 < 3.84, not significant. STEP 6 coverage: 45 of 60 ill people (75%) ate the chicken. CONCLUSION: chicken is the most likely vehicle (highest AR among eaters, lowest among non-eaters, highest RR, significant, most ill people ate it); cake shows no association; rice is weak and not significant. NEXT: confirm with the lab (stool cultures, leftover chicken), check the cooking and holding steps, draw the epi curve (point source if one sharp peak), exclude ill food handlers, recall/discard, communicate findings. ANSWER TEMPLATE for free response: name the measure and why (design), write the formula, substitute, give the number with units, say what it means in plain words, state the conclusion and caveat (association is not proof; check confounders, bias, and the Hill criteria).

WHAT EPIDEMIOLOGY IS. Epidemiology = the study of the DISTRIBUTION (who, where, when - descriptive) and DETERMINANTS (causes and risk factors - analytic) of health-related states or events in specified POPULATIONS, and the APPLICATION of that knowledge to control disease (focus is groups, not diagnosing/treating one patient). Reasons to investigate outbreaks: control and prevention, research/learning, training, public/political/legal concern, program considerations. Types: CLASSICAL (distribution and determinants in populations), CLINICAL (applies it to prevention, detection, treatment in clinical settings), INFECTIOUS disease (needs lab support), CHRONIC disease (complex sampling and statistics), DESCRIPTIVE (person, place, time), ANALYTIC (case-control or cohort, hypothesis testing, causes and effects; hallmark = a comparison group), OCCUPATIONAL (workers exposed to chemical, biological, physical agents). Clinical approach = individual patient, diagnosis and treatment (giving one patient a vaccine; taking blood pressure; prescribing); public health approach = whole population, prevention and policy (smoking ban, recall of lettuce, quarantine, immunization campaign). Public health functions: assessment (find and describe problems), policy development, assurance (carry out and evaluate programs). First step in problem solving: define the problem.

KEY VOCABULARY. Etiology = study of cause. Determinant = any factor (event, characteristic) that changes health; health determinants: genes/biology, health behaviors, social/total ecology (largest), health services. Index case = first case that comes to the attention of health authorities (patient zero as documented); primary case = first person actually infected who brought it in; secondary case = infected by a case. Attrition = people leaving a study (death, disinterest, moving; causes selection bias). Correlation does not equal causation. Confounder = outside variable associated with BOTH exposure and outcome and NOT on the causal pathway, making a false (or hiding a real) association; control by randomization, restriction, matching (design) or stratification, standardization, regression, Mantel-Haenszel (analysis). Effect modification = true effect differs by stratum. Simpson's paradox = crude and stratified results disagree. Infectious = caused by an organism; contagious = spreads person to person; noninfectious (noncommunicable) = genetics, defects, aging, environment (cancer, heart disease, diabetes). Morbidity = illness; mortality = death; natality = births. Risk = probability an individual develops disease in a stated time. Demographic transition = shift from high birth and death rates to low; epidemiologic transition (Omran) = infectious to chronic disease. Preventable risk factors: alcohol, smoking, diet, inactivity, poor sleep; non-preventable: age, genetics, sex, family history, ethnicity.

TRIADS AND CHAIN (diagrams). The epidemiologic triad corners are agent, host, environment (a vector is not a separate corner and "behavior" is not a corner). Chain of transmission (short form): external agent, carrier (fomite or vector), vulnerable host. Example TB: reservoir infected human; exit respiratory tract (cough); airborne droplet nuclei; entry respiratory tract; susceptible host.

TYPES OF OCCURRENCE (-EMICS). Cluster = aggregation of cases closely grouped in time and place (does not require "more than expected" and is the smallest event). Outbreak = more cases than expected in a given area and time (usually local; 40 salmonella cases from one meal). Epidemic = rapid spread to many hosts in a larger area/short time (200 Marburg cases in several districts). Pandemic = epidemic over multiple countries/continents (Black Death 1346-1353, 1918 flu, COVID-19). Sporadic = infrequent, irregular, no pattern. Endemic = constant usual presence in an area (the baseline; malaria in parts of Africa; common cold). Hyperendemic = persistently high in ALL age groups (also used for a persistently high rate); holoendemic = high prevalence in children year-round, adults largely immune (malaria); hypoendemic = low incidence, small affected population; syndemic = 2+ epidemics interact and worsen each other (obesity + diabetes + heart disease; Lyme + co-infections; synergistic). Endemic diseases can still have outbreaks. Plague = life-threatening flea-borne disease (Yersinia pestis) with three forms: bubonic (most common; swollen buboes), septicemic (blood), pneumonic (lungs; spread by droplets).

DISEASE TRANSMISSION (tree in the diagram). Nosocomial = acquired in a hospital (a patient may arrive incubating); zoonosis = animal to human (anthroponosis = human to animal); iatrogenic = caused by medical treatment or procedure. Examples by route: skin, sex and kissing (mononucleosis, gonorrhea, rabies bite, hookworm from soil); droplet within about 1 meter (pertussis, meningococcal disease, flu; CDC counts droplet as direct); vertical = mother to baby (HIV, rubella, Zika, syphilis); fecal-oral (poor hygiene or sanitation); airborne particles under 5 micrometres travel farther (measles, TB, COVID); vehicleborne = food, water, blood (hepatitis A, botulism in improperly canned food, salmonella); fomite = inanimate object (the comb is the fomite and the lice are the agent); waterborne (cholera, dysentery, E. coli). Vector = living animal that carries the agent but does not itself cause the disease (MECHANICAL carries it on its body: flies with Shigella, fleas with Yersinia; BIOLOGICAL = the agent develops in it: malaria in Anopheles, Lyme in Ixodes ticks, guinea worm in copepods); vehicle = non-living carrier. CARRIERS: incubatory (infectious before symptoms), convalescent (infectious after recovery, often thinking themselves cured), transient/temporary (short time), chronic (long time; Typhoid Mary, Salmonella Typhi, Mary Mallon: Irish cook, 53 cases), genetic (carries an inherited trait without symptoms), passive (mechanical carrier). A symptom-free person can still be infectious.

AGENT CHARACTERISTICS (use the right denominator). INFECTIVITY = ability to cause infection = exposed people who become infected / exposed = a/(a+b). PATHOGENICITY = infected people who develop clinical (apparent) disease / infected. VIRULENCE = severity: apparent cases that are severe or fatal / apparent cases (morbidity and mortality). Iceberg phenomenon = only a small proportion of infections are clinically apparent. Spectrum of disease = full range from subclinical through mild, severe, disability, to death.

STAGES OF DISEASE (natural history). The stage order is in the natural-history diagram; the SUBCLINICAL stage is DORMANT if the agent can be recovered and LATENT if it cannot be recovered directly. INCUBATION PERIOD = exposure to first symptoms (acute infection, agent replicating). LATENT PERIOD = exposure to infectiousness; in chronic disease "latency" = exposure to symptoms (Hiroshima exposure to leukemia years later). PRODROMAL = early warning signs before obvious illness (fastigium = peak of fever/symptoms; defervescence = fever falling). A sign is a disease indicator a clinician observes; a symptom is felt by the patient. Prophylaxis = action to prevent disease; fulminant = sudden, severe onset.

CASE DEFINITION AND SURVEILLANCE. A case definition has 4 parts: CLINICAL information (symptoms, lab confirmation), PERSON (age, sex, race, occupation), PLACE (geographic or facility), TIME (specific period). Example: "any person with vomiting or diarrhea who attended the event on [date]". Levels: SUSPECTED/POSSIBLE (symptoms only), PROBABLE (compatible illness plus epidemiologic link, no lab), CONFIRMED (lab-confirmed); not a case. Early in an outbreak use a broad, SENSITIVE definition; later a narrow, specific one. CDC food-outbreak steps: detect (monitor reports), find (case definition and look for more cases), generate hypotheses (interview food histories), test (compare people with and without the exposure), solve (confirm the contaminated food and point of contamination), control (recall, sanitation, advice), decide (outbreak over when no new illness and no contaminated food). SURVEILLANCE = systematic, ongoing collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination of health data in order to prevent and control. Five steps: identify/define/measure the health problem; collect data; analyze and interpret; provide data and interpretation to health authorities (disseminate); monitor and evaluate the system (and take action). Alternate version: data collection, analysis, interpretation, distribution, action (surveillance alone does not always include control actions). (Passive, active, sentinel and syndromic surveillance: see the table.) Notifiable disease = CDC/CSTE list (reported to state health departments; botulism and lead are notifiable to find a common source); reportable = must be reported to local or state health departments. Surveillance attributes: timeliness, sensitivity, representativeness, simplicity, flexibility, acceptability, predictive value positive. Data sources: vital records, notifiable disease reports, hospital data, surveys; RCTs are not surveillance. Agencies: CDC (founded July 1, 1946 as the Communicable Disease Center, Atlanta; Joseph Mountin; HQ Atlanta, part of HHS), WHO (UN health agency, HQ Geneva; promotes health, security, systems, evidence), MMWR (CDC weekly), NNDSS (national notifiable disease system), FoodNet (active foodborne surveillance), NORS, NARMS, FluSurv-NET, NREVSS, CaliciNet (norovirus), PulseNet (below). Reasons incidence may rise falsely: better reporting system, new test, publicity, case definition change, lab error, false positives, population change, newly notifiable.

OUTBREAK INVESTIGATION - THE STEPS. (The CDC 10-step and 13-step ladders are in the diagram; if exactly 10 steps are asked use the CDC 10-step list; testing a hypothesis MUST have a control group.) A line listing = spreadsheet with one row per case and columns for variables (person, place, time, symptoms, lab). Early interviews generate hypotheses; a hypothesis is tested with a comparison group; use a cohort study when the population is known (wedding guests, team) and a case-control when it is large or undefined. Outbreak graphs: epi curve = histogram of cases by onset time (types in the math section: point source, continuous, intermittent, propagated); line chart, bar graph, scatter plot also used. Age pattern of deaths: seasonal flu U-shaped (infants and elderly), 1918 flu W-shaped (young adults died too).

FOODBORNE ILLNESS. FATTOM (conditions pathogens need): Food (protein-rich), Acidity (pH 6.6-7.5 best; FDA requires below 4.5 to stop growth), Time (do not leave food in the danger zone more than 2-4 hours), Temperature (danger zone 41-135 F; poultry 165 F, ground meat 160 F), Oxygen (most are aerobic; C. botulinum is anaerobic and grows in low-acid canned food), Moisture (water activity aw 0-1.0; pathogens thrive at 0.95-1.0; FDA requires below 0.85). Preservation by salt, sugar, drying, freezing, acid works by cutting water or lowering pH. Prevention: clean, separate (avoid cross-contamination), cook, chill; cook meat, poultry and eggs thoroughly; refrigerate leftovers; wash produce; report suspected foodborne illness to the health department. Foodborne INTOXICATION = preformed toxin (S. aureus in potato or egg salad, B. cereus, C. perfringens), fast onset 30 min-8 h, no fever, live bacteria not needed, antibiotics useless; foodborne INFECTION = live organisms multiply in gut (Salmonella, Campylobacter, E. coli, Listeria). Top US foodborne germs: norovirus (most common cause of foodborne illness; low infectious dose; vomiting, diarrhea; incubation 12-48 h, median about 33 h; peaks in winter; spread by food handlers, shellfish, produce, surfaces; control with soap handwashing, bleach, excluding sick food workers 48 h after symptoms; no vaccine; diagnosed by RT-PCR), Salmonella, C. perfringens, Campylobacter, S. aureus. Food-pathogen matches: botulism = home-canned vegetables; Listeria = deli meats, hot dogs, soft cheese, unpasteurized dairy (survives refrigeration; dangerous in pregnancy); E. coli O157:H7/STEC = undercooked ground beef, produce (Shiga toxin, hemolytic uremic syndrome; do NOT give antibiotics); Vibrio vulnificus/parahaemolyticus = raw shellfish, seawater; cyclospora = fresh produce; Salmonella = eggs, poultry, raw milk, reptiles, turtles (4-inch law 1975), backyard poultry; Campylobacter = poultry, raw milk, undercooked chicken liver; hepatitis A = frozen strawberries (1997), green onions; cholera = water; Cryptosporidium = water (Milwaukee 1993). Known outbreaks: Jack in the Box 1993 E. coli O157:H7; Blue Bell 2015 Listeria; Iowa eggs 2010 Salmonella Enteritidis; Chipotle 2015 E. coli. PulseNet = CDC network of labs that compares the DNA fingerprints of bacteria (PFGE band patterns, now whole-genome sequencing) to link cases across states; works for bacteria, not for viruses or prions. Pasteurization = brief heat; FSIS (USDA) regulates meat, poultry, eggs; FDA the rest; HACCP controls hazards, FSMA/HARPC emphasizes prevention. Safe-handling basics: thaw in refrigerator, cold water or microwave only.

ENVIRONMENTAL HEALTH. Environmental quality = biological, chemical and physical causes of illness (pollution, smoking, disasters, toxins, pesticides); WHO: about 24% of disease is caused by environmental factors. AIR: ground-level ozone (asthma, poor lung function, premature death) comes from precursor chemicals, methane, wildfires, stagnation; particle pollution = dust, dirt, soot from primary sources (wood stove, which pollute directly) and secondary sources (cars, form particles from gases); air pollution causes COPD. Lead: old paint, leaded gasoline, pipes, batteries; blood lead threshold 10 ug/dL (now lower); lead lowers IQ. Pesticides and metals bioaccumulate (build up in one organism) and biomagnify (rise at each higher trophic level; long-lived predatory fish such as tuna and swordfish carry the most mercury; Minamata disease = methylmercury). Clean water: boiling, chlorination, filtration, UV; floods promote cholera; acute diarrhea treatment = oral rehydration + zinc; rotavirus is the leading global cause of diarrhea in children. Mosquito control: remove standing water, screens, bed nets with insecticide, repellents. Climate change widens mosquito and tick ranges. Motor vehicle safety: seat belts, airbags, child seats. Top CDC public health challenges: alcohol-related harms, food safety, healthcare-associated infections, heart disease/stroke, HIV, motor vehicle injuries, nutrition/physical activity/obesity, prescription drug overdose, teen pregnancy, tobacco. Leading cause of death in the US: heart disease.

IMMUNITY AND VACCINES. ACTIVE immunity: exposure to a live pathogen or vaccine; own immune response, memory cells (long-lasting). PASSIVE immunity: antibodies made elsewhere, short term (gamma globulin, antitoxin/antivenom, maternal antibodies crossing the placenta or in milk = natural passive). ARTIFICIAL immunity = deliberate exposure (vaccine). HERD IMMUNITY: when enough people are immune the chain breaks and even the unvaccinated (infants, immunocompromised) are indirectly protected; threshold HIT = (R0 - 1)/R0 (higher R0 needs more people immune; pockets of low coverage allow local outbreaks). HIV kills helper T (CD4) cells; U=U: HIV undetectable for 6 months cannot be transmitted sexually; HIV is the virus, AIDS the late stage. Inactivated (killed) vs live attenuated vaccines; toxoid; conjugate; mRNA (cold chain, rare myocarditis). Antigenic drift = small point mutations (seasonal flu, yearly vaccine, partial immunity); antigenic shift = major reassortment (pandemic strain). Antigen = foreign substance that triggers an immune response; antibody = blood protein that counters a specific antigen. Quarantine = restricts movement of well people who MAY have been exposed; isolation = separates SICK people. Contact tracing = find and monitor exposed contacts. Control vocabulary: control (reduce to an acceptable level, needs continued effort), elimination of disease or infection, eradication (permanent worldwide zero; smallpox is the only human disease eradicated; polio wild virus remains in Afghanistan and Pakistan), extinction. Bacteriophage = virus that infects bacteria; virophage needs a co-infecting giant virus. Bioterrorism = deliberate release of live agents. Arbovirus = arthropod-borne virus (dengue, West Nile, chikungunya, Zika, yellow fever). Exogenous infection = from contact with a contaminated surface; endogenous = the pathogen already colonized the body (nose).

PREVENTION LEVELS (diagram). QUATERNARY = protect from unnecessary or excessive medical intervention (antibiotic stewardship, avoiding over-treatment). Prevention before symptoms = primary + secondary. Screening works when the disease is serious, early treatment helps, the test is acceptable and accurate, prevalence is high enough, and follow-up care exists.

STUDY DESIGNS (pros and cons). EXPERIMENTAL: investigator assigns the exposure and tracks outcomes; RCT/clinical trial (human experimentation with controlled exposure, randomization, usually blinding; gold standard for causation; most scientifically sound and best control of exposure; cons: time-consuming, very expensive, unethical for harmful exposures, restricted generalizability, risk can cause disinterest); single-blind (subjects), double-blind (subjects and researchers), triple-blind (+ analysts); community trial = whole community is the study group; field trial; QUASI-experiment = no randomization (more generalizable, weaker causal conclusions). OBSERVATIONAL: investigators only observe. COHORT: gives incidence and RR, good for rare exposures and multiple outcomes, correct time sequence; cons: slow, expensive, attrition, bad for rare diseases; PROSPECTIVE (followed forward from before outcomes) or RETROSPECTIVE (past records; two groups identified after the fact; may lack confounder data). CASE-CONTROL: gives OR; cheap, fast, good for rare disease and long latency, multiple exposures; cons: recall bias, selection of controls, only one outcome, no incidence or RR, time order unclear; controls must come from the same at-risk source population. CROSS-SECTIONAL (survey): gives prevalence; fastest, cheapest, multiple outcomes; cons: cannot separate incidence from survival, no temporality, least confident. ECOLOGICAL: groups, not individuals (ecological fallacy = applying a group association to individuals). NATURAL experiment: nature assigns exposure (Snow's water companies). Case report (one patient), case series (several, no comparison group). Descriptive designs: case reports, case series, cross-sectional, ecological. Hierarchy of evidence (low to high): case report < ecological < cross-sectional < case-control < cohort < RCT < systematic review/meta-analysis (meta-analysis combines independent studies). GRADE rates certainty of evidence; RCTs start high; it is moved DOWN for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias and UP for large effect and dose-response; strength of association and dose-response matter. PICO = Population, Intervention, Control, Outcome; FINER = feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant. Sequential testing = sample size not fixed in advance; boundaries narrow as data accumulate. Internal validity = valid within the study; external validity = generalizable; random selection improves generalizability, not internal validity.

CAUSATION. BRADFORD HILL (9 criteria in the diagram): TEMPORALITY is the only absolutely required one; dose-response = biological gradient; coherence = findings agree with laboratory/biological knowledge and past studies (a lab strain match of the food agent counts here or as experiment). The old notes listed "alternative explanations" in place of analogy; CDC/other lists of "six" use: temporal relationship, strength, dose-response, replication/consistency, biologic plausibility, consideration of alternative explanations. KOCH'S POSTULATES (4, in the diagram; they need a pure culture). Limits: healthy carriers (Typhoid Mary) break 1; viruses, prions and obligate intracellular bacteria (Chlamydia, Rickettsia) cannot be pure-cultured (2); non-infectious causes do not fit; "asymptomatic after treatment" is not a postulate. EVANS' POSTULATES (10, 1976; Koch and Hill plus host response; list in the diagram). ROTHMAN'S CAUSAL PIES: sufficient cause = the whole pie; necessary cause = appears in every pie; component cause = one slice. Web of causation; DAG (directed acyclic graph) maps causal and confounding paths. Efficiency = results per resources; efficacy (ideal conditions) vs effectiveness (real conditions). Hormesis = low-dose stimulation, high-dose inhibition. Reduce confounding by randomization, restriction, matching, then stratification and regression. Matching drawbacks: cannot study the matched variable, overmatching, matched analysis needed.

ERROR AND BIAS (map in the diagram). Random error = chance fluctuation (shrinks with larger samples; precision = inverse of random error); systematic error = consistent error (bias; a larger sample does not fix it; accuracy/validity). Selection, information and confounding bias and the named biases are in the bias map. Extras: attrition, non-response and undercoverage (landline-only surveys), healthy volunteer bias, referral bias, exclusion bias; surrogate interviewer bias (next of kin remember only good things), family information bias, reporting and social-desirability bias, differential misclassification (unequal by exposure group; non-differential pushes toward no association). Recall bias is reduced by objective records or sick controls. Surveillance/detection bias = a monitored group is checked more thoroughly. Temporal bias = unknown timeline of exposure and outcome. Lead-time bias: screening finds disease earlier so survival APPEARS longer while mortality is unchanged; length-time bias: screening finds slow, less aggressive tumors. Protopathic bias = a drug is given for early undiagnosed disease. Pygmalion/Rosenthal effect = researcher expectations alter results; panel effect = repeated surveys of the same people. Publication bias; aggregation (ecological) bias. Healthy-worker effect = workers are healthier than the general population, so occupational death rates look falsely low. Type 3 error = right answer to the wrong question; Type 4 = wrong question or misinterpretation (Type 1, Type 2 and power are in the statistics section).

RATE VOCABULARY SHORT LIST. Morbidity rate = new cases/population; mortality rate = deaths/population; birth rate = births per year/midyear population; fertility rate = live births per 1,000 women aged 15-44; death rate = deaths per year/midyear population; case fatality rate = deaths from disease/people with disease; cause-specific mortality = deaths from the disease/population; proportionate mortality = deaths from cause in the target group/all deaths. Risk of illness is treated as incidence ("x cases / y population / z time"). Incidence suits cohort studies and acute diseases; prevalence suits cross-sectional studies. Point prevalence = at one time; period prevalence = over a duration. Secondary attack rate = infections among susceptible close contacts (household), showing how social interaction affects transmission risk. EFFICACY old-notes confusion: vaccine effectiveness = 1 - (attack rate vaccinated/attack rate unvaccinated); it is NOT "new cases/total population x 1000" (that is incidence).

HISTORY AND PEOPLE (match the person to the deed). HIPPOCRATES (about 460-370 BC): father of modern medicine; first to explain disease rationally by environment and lifestyle; recorded disease in different populations. JOHN GRAUNT (1600s UK): father of demography and vital statistics; analyzed London's weekly Bills of Mortality (1662), used ratios and the rule of three to estimate population, birth and death rates, saw male/female differences, began statistical association and inference; doubted plague death reports; investigated 1634 deaths blamed on "rickets". THOMAS SYDENHAM (1624-89): recorded observations of disease apart from Hippocratic theory, separated fevers (continued, intermittent) and smallpox in London. JAMES LIND (1716-94): Scottish naval surgeon; experimental trial of 6 treatment pairs on sailors with scurvy; citrus cured them in 6 days (first controlled clinical trial); lime juice added to rations, sailors called "limeys". EDWARD JENNER (1749-1823): cowpox used to make the first smallpox vaccine (1796); Benjamin Jesty inoculated earlier. IGNAZ SEMMELWEIS (1800s): Vienna maternity clinic with high childbed (puerperal) fever death in the doctors' clinic; physicians moved from autopsies to pelvic exams; he required handwashing in chlorinated lime. JOHN SNOW (1813-58): anesthesiologist and founder of modern/field epidemiology; 1854 London cholera, Broad Street pump in Soho, compared water companies (natural experiment), removed the pump handle; rejected miasma. WILLIAM FARR (1807-83): founder of medical statistics, England's cause-of-death system, foundations of surveillance. LOUIS PASTEUR (1822-95): germ theory, disproved spontaneous generation, pasteurization, anthrax and rabies vaccines. ROBERT KOCH (1843-1910): postulates; anthrax, TB, cholera. PERCIVALL POTT: scrotal cancer in chimney sweeps (first occupational carcinogen). WALTER REED: yellow fever and mosquitoes. EDGAR SYDENSTRICKER: first morbidity study (Hagerstown, Maryland, 1921-24). JOSEPH GOLDBERGER: pellagra is a niacin (diet) deficiency. ALEXANDER FLEMING: accidentally discovered penicillin (1928). ALEXANDER LANGMUIR (1910-93): key to developing CDC, applied epidemiology and surveillance. JOSEPH MOUNTIN: founded the CDC (July 1, 1946; as the Communicable Disease Center, to fight malaria). MARY MALLON (Typhoid Mary, 1900s): Irish cook, healthy chronic carrier of Salmonella Typhi, responsible for about 50 (old notes: 53) cases of typhoid in about 15 years, 3 deaths; led to protection of public food supplies. FRAMINGHAM (Massachusetts, 1948): prospective cohort on cardiovascular risk factors; about 5,000 people aged 30-62 (original 6,000); 30-year study. RICHARD DOLL and BRADFORD HILL (1951): first cohort study linking smoking and lung cancer (smokers about 10 times more likely to die of it); Hill's nine criteria. Surgeon General's smoking report 1964. JONAS SALK: largest vaccine trial, inactivated polio vaccine (1950s); ALBERT SABIN: oral polio vaccine (1961). PAUL FARMER: co-founded Partners in Health, built hospital in Haiti after 2010 earthquake. PARACELSUS: father of toxicology. FREDERICK SANGER: dideoxy (Sanger) sequencing. ALICE STEWART: X-rays in pregnancy and childhood cancer (Oxford Survey); BRIAN MacMAHON: modern epidemiology text on study design; THEOBALD SMITH: hypothesis that pathogens evolve toward lower virulence (not always true: COVID spreads pre-symptomatically, fungi live in the environment). IBN AL-NAFIS: pulmonary circulation. Reed-Frost = chain-binomial model. Kermack and McKendrick (1927): SIR model. 1976 Yambuku Ebola: reused unsterilized needles, contact with body fluids and corpses; Legionnaires disease first recognized in Philadelphia 1976 (American Legion convention); Reston 1989 monkeys = Ebola Reston; Four Corners 1993 = hantavirus; NYC 1999 = West Nile; Omaha 1999 meatpackers = rubella; Wakefield 1998 MMR-autism paper: 12 children, no control group, fraud, retracted; vaccines do not cause autism. Black Death killed on the order of 75-200 million (Yersinia pestis, fleas, rodents). Texas sharpshooter fallacy = drawing the target around cases after the fact (cluster artifacts).

DISEASE CARDS (agent; transmission; key points). CHOLERA: Vibrio cholerae (Gram-negative bacterium); fecal-oral/water; rice-water diarrhea, treat with ORS (oral rehydration), CFR 1-2% (5.7% in Zimbabwe 2008 for lack of care); Snow. TUBERCULOSIS: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (acid-fast); airborne droplet nuclei; latent TB = no symptoms, normal chest X-ray, not contagious; TST read at 48-72 h; treat latent infection with isoniazid 6-9 months; notifiable. MALARIA: Plasmodium falciparum, vivax, ovale, malariae, knowlesi (protozoa); female Anopheles bite (biological vector); incubation 7-30 d (vivax/ovale hypnozoites sleep in liver); bed nets, standing-water control, prophylaxis; vaccine hard because the parasite hides and changes antigens. LYME: Borrelia burgdorferi (spirochete); Ixodes (black-legged) tick; reservoir white-footed mouse (deer are tick hosts but not competent; humans dead-end); nymphs cause most cases; erythema migrans rash 3-30 d; early tests may be negative; dilution effect (loss of biodiversity raises risk). PLAGUE: Yersinia pestis; flea bite (Xenopsylla), pneumonic by droplet. ANTHRAX: Bacillus anthracis (spore-forming Gram-positive); livestock/herbivores; cutaneous, inhalation, ingestion. BOTULISM: Clostridium botulinum toxin; canned food; infant botulism from honey; descending paralysis. TETANUS: Clostridium tetani, wounds, not person-to-person. LISTERIOSIS: Listeria monocytogenes; deli meats, soft cheese. LEGIONELLA: aerosolized water, cooling towers. TYPHOID: Salmonella Typhi; fecal-oral, carriers. SALMONELLOSIS: Salmonella (Enteritidis, Typhimurium, Newport); eggs, poultry, reptiles; incubation 6-72 h. E. COLI/STEC: Shiga toxin, HUS (hemolytic anemia, kidney failure, low platelets); incubation 1-10 d (mean 4). SHIGELLOSIS: fecal-oral, flies mechanical vector. CAMPYLOBACTER: C. jejuni, poultry, most common bacterial diarrhea cause. STAPH FOOD POISONING: S. aureus enterotoxin, onset 30 min-8 h (average 3 h), no fever. MRSA: methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, direct contact; tests call 50-70% of MRSA hospital-acquired (nosocomial). STREP: Streptococcus pyogenes; droplet; also scarlet and rheumatic fever. PERTUSSIS: Bordetella pertussis; droplet; waning acellular vaccine immunity. DIPHTHERIA: Corynebacterium. MENINGOCOCCAL: Neisseria meningitidis; droplet; vaccine; dorm outbreaks. PSITTACOSIS: Chlamydia psittaci from birds. RMSF: Rickettsia rickettsii (tick); EHRLICHIOSIS: tick. TULAREMIA: Francisella (ticks, rabbits). Q FEVER: Coxiella. GAS GANGRENE: Clostridium perfringens. FUNGI: candidiasis (Candida), aspergillosis, histoplasmosis (inhaled spores from bat/bird droppings; not bacterial). MUTATIONS AND GENETICS TERMS. Mutations occur during cell division: substitution, deletion, insertion. Silent/synonymous (point) mutation = no change in amino acid; missense/nonsynonymous = one amino acid changed; nonsense = new stop codon. Antigenic drift (minor) and shift (major). SNP = single-nucleotide polymorphism; GWAS = genome-wide association study (case-control, Manhattan plot; marker SNPs near the causal gene by linkage disequilibrium). Penetrance = fraction with the genotype who show the trait; expressivity = degree of expression.

LAB AND DATA TERMS. ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) detects antibodies or antigens; rapid antigen tests show CURRENT infection, antibody tests show past exposure and miss early infection; RT-PCR/PCR amplifies genetic material (gold standard for norovirus, COVID); stool culture confirms bacterial foodborne illness; Gram stain: thick peptidoglycan retains crystal violet = Gram-positive (purple), thin = Gram-negative (pink); bacteria are prokaryotes (no nucleus), bacilli are rods, cocci spheres, spirochetes spirals; endospores survive harsh conditions; antibiotics do not work on viruses; bacteriostatic stops growth, bactericidal kills; endotoxin = lipopolysaccharide of Gram-negative cell walls, exotoxin = secreted. Biosafety levels: BSL-1 harmless E. coli, BSL-2 Salmonella, S. aureus, HIV, BSL-3 TB, West Nile, SARS-CoV, BSL-4 Ebola, Marburg, Lassa (smallpox stocks at CDC Atlanta and a Russian lab). ROC curve: a curve near the top-left corner has area (AUC) near 1 = accurate; AUC of a perfect test = 1.0. FIGO cancer stages: I confined to organ of origin, II invasion of nearby organs or tissue, III spread to distant nodes or tissue within the pelvis, IV distant metastasis. Scientific method: observe, question, research, hypothesize, experiment, test hypothesis, conclude, report. Clinical interview collects behaviors, attitudes, situation, personality, life history. Meta-analysis = pools results of independent studies. PulseNet concept: analyze bacterial DNA to find where else the strain is. Symptom vs sign; Quarantine vs isolation (above). Sufficient cause / necessary cause (Rothman). Notifiable vs reportable. U=U. Health Institutions: CDC protects America from health, safety and security threats, conducts experiments, informs the public, responds to crises (part of the US Department of Health and Human Services, Atlanta); WHO (Geneva); APHA (American Public Health Association); NIOSH (occupational safety and health); FDA, USDA/FSIS, HHS.

POLICY AND OTHER. Disease control policy steps: problem identification, policy analysis, strategy and policy development, policy enactment. Public health policy = laws, regulations, actions and decisions that promote wellness and meet health goals. Top public health concerns: obesity, cancer, diabetes, mental health, climate change, equity. Health disparity = preventable differences in disease burden among groups. WHO definition of health: complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely absence of disease. Hygiene hypothesis: early microbial exposure (farm children) lowers asthma and allergy. Cluster investigation caution: counts are not rates (compare New Mexico and Pennsylvania with rates per 100,000 and age adjustment). Healthy-user bias: HRT users seemed to have less heart disease, later disproved by RCTs. TB contact investigation: concentric circles by risk, TST repeated 8-10 weeks after exposure. LIKELY TEST FACTS TO KNOW COLD. Gold standard of evidence for causation: RCT. Only essential Hill criterion: temporality. Study with no control group: case series. Hospital controls: Berkson bias; remembering exposures differently: recall bias; earlier detection looks like longer survival: lead-time bias. Clinical vs public health: individual vs population. Outbreak vs epidemic vs pandemic vs endemic vs sporadic vs cluster (above). Quaternary prevention = avoid over-medicalization. Point source curve = single peak; propagated = growing waves. Smallpox: only eradicated human disease. Polio: remaining in Pakistan and Afghanistan. Typhoid Mary: asymptomatic carrier. Koch postulates need pure culture. DALY = YLL + YLD.

MORE MATH RULES FOUND BY PRACTICE TESTS. SERIES testing (both must be positive): overall sensitivity = Se1 x Se2 and overall specificity = 1 - (1-Sp1)(1-Sp2); PARALLEL (either positive): sensitivity = 1 - (1-Se1)(1-Se2), specificity = Sp1 x Sp2; the second test's PPV/NPV uses only the people referred on (first-test positives) as its N. Specificity = TN/(TN+FP) where the denominator is everyone truly non-diseased by the gold standard. Subgroup risk factors (rates by age, sex, deck, emote, food): compare each group's rate with the reference group (RR = group rate/reference rate); RR > 1 risk factor, the larger the stronger; check each subgroup (sex pattern too). OR approximates RR when the disease is rare, cases represent all cases and controls represent all non-diseased. To cut Re from 1.5 to 1 with R0 = 12: susceptible fraction falls from 1.5/12 = 12.5% to 1/12 = 8.33%, so an extra 4.17% of the population must be immune; R0 = contacts x P(transmission) (24 contacts, R0 12 gives P = 0.5). Earlier screening with no cure: true incidence unchanged but diagnosed time (lead time) longer, so prevalence and apparent survival rise, mortality unchanged (lead-time bias) - not an incidence rise. If a stem's table totals do not match its text (patients, sick counts), compute from the cell values and state the discrepancy. "Less than 5% probability the result is due to chance" is the usual accepted p < 0.05 wording. Select-all items: only tick options directly consistent with the definition (P = I x D: lower incidence cannot give higher prevalence unless duration rises).

DESIGN, PREVENTION AND CONTROL RULES. Outbreak with a known guest list = retrospective cohort (attack rates, RR) even if the table lists ill vs well; case-control only if controls were sampled separately (OR); risk factors for an outcome that already happened = case-control. Cohort aliases: longitudinal, prospective, follow-up, incidence study. Nested case-control: controls sampled from cohort members still at risk when each case occurs; case-cohort: a random subcohort picked at baseline serves many outcomes; both are cheaper, measure exposure before disease (less recall bias), and suit rare outcomes. Case-crossover: each case is its own control (hazard window vs earlier window) for transient exposures and acute outcomes. Ecological time trend = historical trends in population rates; a single-arm uncontrolled intervention = clinical trial; a "cohort" where only cases are interviewed = case series. Case-control = retrospective only; cohort = prospective or retrospective. Elimination of an existing hazard or banning a supplier = primary prevention; primordial = prevent the risk factor from arising (policy, built environment); naloxone after overdose = tertiary; screening such as blood pressure checks = secondary. Control strategies: source removal/reduction (eliminate the reservoir or contaminated source: recall food, confiscate drug supply, remediate soil or water), host protection (vaccine, prophylaxis), break transmission (hygiene, masks, distancing), isolation (sick) and quarantine (exposed), surveillance and education; environmental remediation suits non-infectious hazards; a non-infectious outbreak = toxin or non-biological agent, not person-to-person. CARRIER stems that give no timing: incubatory if before symptoms, convalescent if after, chronic if long-term (Typhoid Mary); some tests use "passive" for any healthy carrier. Sentinel surveillance = designated sites report regularly (a type of enhanced/active). Outbreak keys sometimes list "find cases" and "describe person, place, time" as separate steps; if exactly 10 steps are asked use the CDC list. Detection bias = testing only symptomatic or severe cases misses mild ones. HAI (hospital infections) rise from more susceptible patients, invasive devices and procedures, and antimicrobial-resistant organisms; TB rises with drug resistance (MDR/XDR), HIV, crowding and poverty, migration, reduced screening, waning BCG. Hill examples: disease only in the exposed = specificity; larger RR = less likely wholly due to bias or confounding; top three usually cited: temporality, strength, consistency or dose-response.

BODY-SYSTEM BIOLOGY SEEN ON TESTS. GUT: the large intestine holds most microbes (10^11-10^12 per gram); Peyer's patches = lymphoid follicles in the small intestine (ileum); M (microfold) cells carry antigens from the gut lumen to Peyer's patches. RESPIRATORY: upper tract has normal flora (colonization resistance: commensals compete for nutrients and attachment sites and make bacteriocins); the lower tract is kept near sterile by the mucociliary escalator, cough, alveolar macrophages and secretory IgA; TB is inhaled in low doses to alveolar macrophages and T cells (granuloma). LIPIDS: cholesterol travels in lipoproteins; LDL = "bad", HDL carries cholesterol back; macrophages engulf oxidized LDL to become foam cells, then fatty streak and plaque (atherosclerosis). VIRAL GENETICS: influenza = segmented negative-sense ssRNA whose polymerase lacks proofreading (frequent mutation = drift; segment reassortment = shift); (-)ssRNA must make mRNA with its own RNA polymerase, (+)ssRNA is itself mRNA, retroviruses go RNA to DNA to mRNA, dsDNA viruses transcribe mRNA from DNA.
