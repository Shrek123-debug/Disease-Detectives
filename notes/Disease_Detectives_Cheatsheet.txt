FIND IT (formula index): attack rate/RR/risk difference/AF% 1-2 | OR, matched OR, Mantel-Haenszel 3 | which measure? | sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, LR, series/parallel 4 | incidence, prevalence, person-time, P = I x D 5 | CFR, mortality family, standardization, SMR, YPLL, DALY 6 | vaccine efficacy, ARR, NNT, NNH 7 | R0, HIT, Re, SIR 8 | chi-square 9 | mean, SD, z 10 | CI, t, p, errors 11 | epi curve and incubation 12 | traps 13 | then concepts: triads, chain, -emics, transmission, stages, case definition, surveillance, outbreak steps, food, environment, immunity, prevention, study designs, causation, bias, history, diseases, lab terms. ONE-LINE FORMULAS: AR = ill/group; RR = [a/(a+b)]/[c/(c+d)]; OR = ad/bc; matched OR = b/c; AF% = (RR-1)/RR; Se = TP/(TP+FN); Sp = TN/(TN+FP); PPV = TP/(TP+FP); NPV = TN/(TN+FN); LR+ = Se/(1-Sp); incidence = new/at risk; prevalence = all cases/population; P = I x D; CFR = deaths/cases; VE = 1 - RR; NNT = 1/ARR; HIT = 1 - 1/R0; Re = R0 x s; R0 = contacts x p x duration; chi-square = sum (O-E)^2/E with E = row x column/N, df 1, critical 3.84; sample SD = sqrt[sum (x-mean)^2/(n-1)]; z = (x-mean)/SD; CI = mean +/- z x SD/sqrt(n); t = (mean - null)/(SD/sqrt n); SMR = observed/expected; YPLL = sum (LE - age at death); DALY = YLL + YLD; exposure window = [first onset - max incubation, last onset - min incubation].

MATH FIRST - HOW TO ATTACK ANY CALCULATION. (1) Underline what is asked and the units (per 100, per 1,000, per 100,000, percent, "to the hundredth"). (2) Write the numbers into the right table or formula before calculating. (3) Keep full calculator precision and round ONLY the final answer, unless the test prints rounded intermediate values. (4) Write the formula, the substitution, the answer WITH units, and one sentence of interpretation in plain words (partial credit is common). (5) Sanity check: a proportion is 0-1 (or 0-100%), RR and OR are never negative, sensitivity/specificity are 0-100%. Typical directions: "round to the nearest hundredth" for RR/OR/ratios, "tenth of a percent" for attack rates and percentages, "per 1,000" or "per 100,000" for population rates, 2-3 significant figures for statistics. Tests often hand you extra distractor numbers (hospitalized counts, total population, number of foods) - use only the numbers the definition needs.

THE UNIVERSAL 2x2 TABLE (draw it every time). Rows = exposure (ate the food / attended / vaccinated / smoker / test positive). Columns = outcome (ill vs well / case vs control / disease vs no disease). Cells: a = exposed AND ill, b = exposed AND well, c = unexposed AND ill, d = unexposed AND well. Row totals: a+b (all exposed), c+d (all unexposed). Column totals: a+c (all ill), b+d (all well). Grand total N = a+b+c+d. DANGER: tests often print the table turned sideways (ill on top, "Case/Control" by "Attended/Did not"), put the unexposed row first, or label columns "Has disease"; RELABEL a, b, c, d yourself before using any formula. Check that a+b+c+d equals the number of people stated (some tests print totals that do not add up - then just compute from the cells as written). When only the exposed row is given (typical of "which food made people sick"), you can compute the attack rate among eaters but not RR.

1. ATTACK RATE (AR) = risk = cumulative incidence in a group followed over a short outbreak. Formula: AR = (number who became ill in the group) / (number of people in that group) x 100. Always divide by the people IN THAT GROUP, never by the total number ill. Exposed AR = a/(a+b); unexposed AR = c/(c+d); overall AR = (a+c)/N. FOOD-SPECIFIC attack rate: for each food, AR among people who ate it vs AR among people who did not eat it. HOW TO FIND THE GUILTY FOOD: make the 2x2 for every food; the culprit has (i) a HIGH attack rate among eaters, (ii) a LOW attack rate among non-eaters, so the biggest difference or biggest ratio, and (iii) almost every ill person ate it (a is nearly all of a+c). If every food has RR close to 1.0 (all between about 0.8 and 1.2), no food is implicated - suspect person-to-person spread or a non-food source. Secondary attack rate (SAR) = new cases among susceptible contacts of an index case / number of susceptible contacts x 100; the index/primary cases are NOT in the denominator (6 sick children go home, 4 of their 10 siblings catch it: SAR = 4/10 = 40%). Example: potluck, ate potato salad: 40 ill, 20 well; did not eat it: 10 ill, 40 well. AR eaters = 40/60 = 66.7%; AR non-eaters = 10/50 = 20.0%. Another: ate salad 30 ill of 40 eaters = 75.0%; non-eaters 10 ill of 60 = 16.7%; ate pie 25 of 50 = 50% vs 15 of 50 = 30%: salad is the culprit (bigger gap). If the table lists foods only for eaters (Spaghetti 22 ill of 58 = 0.379; Caviar 52 of 86 = 0.605; Steak 12 of 52 = 0.231) the highest eater AR (caviar) is named the culprit and the measure asked is "attack rate".

2. RELATIVE RISK (RR) = risk ratio = attack-rate ratio. Formula: RR = [a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)] = (AR exposed)/(AR unexposed). Use for COHORT designs (known groups, e.g. everyone at a wedding, everyone who got the vaccine or not, clinical trials, retrospective cohorts of a defined guest list) because you know the size of both groups. Interpretation: RR = 1 no association; RR > 1 exposure is a risk factor ("exposed people were RR times as likely to get ill"); RR < 1 exposure is PROTECTIVE (never say "negative risk"). Same potluck example: RR = 66.7% / 20.0% = 3.33 (people who ate it were 3.33 times as likely to become ill). Another: 36/120 = 0.30 vs 9/90 = 0.10, RR = 3.0. If AR in exposed is 43% and unexposed 54%, RR = 0.79: the "suspect" water looks protective - do not force a cause. Say "2.5 times as likely" NOT "2.5% higher". RR cannot be computed from a case-control study. Risk difference = attributable risk = AR exposed - AR unexposed (extra cases per 100 exposed; potluck 66.7 - 20.0 = 46.7 percentage points). Attributable fraction among the exposed (AF% or AR%) = (AR exposed - AR unexposed)/AR exposed = (RR-1)/RR x 100 (potluck: (3.33-1)/3.33 = 70.0%: 70% of the exposed's illness is due to the exposure). Population attributable risk = AR in whole population - AR in unexposed; PAF% = that / AR in whole population.

3. ODDS RATIO (OR). Formula: OR = (a x d)/(b x c) (cross-product ratio) = [odds of exposure in cases]/[odds of exposure in controls] = [odds of disease in exposed]/[odds of disease in unexposed]. Use for CASE-CONTROL studies (people picked because they have the disease, plus comparison people without it) - there the group sizes are chosen by the investigator, so risks and RR are meaningless; the OR is the only valid measure. Layout for case-control: columns = cases | controls, rows = exposed | unexposed; a = exposed cases, b = exposed controls, c = unexposed cases, d = unexposed controls. Steps: (1) label cells, (2) a x d, (3) b x c, (4) divide. Example (potluck case-control): cases attended 65, did not attend 2; controls attended 43, did not 18: OR = (65 x 18)/(2 x 43) = 1170/86 = 13.60 - the odds of attending were 13.6 times higher in cases than in controls (strong association). The same potluck data would give a wrong "RR" of 6.0. Potluck cohort table above: OR = (40 x 40)/(20 x 10) = 8.00 (RR was 3.33: OR is farther from 1 than RR when the outcome is common). Interpretation words: "the odds of exposure were OR times higher among cases"; for a CASE-CONTROL OR say odds, not "times more likely to get the disease" unless the disease is rare. Rare-disease assumption: when disease is rare (about under 10%) a and c are tiny so OR approximates RR. OR < 1 protective; OR = 1 none. If any cell is 0, OR is undefined/infinite (no control was exposed). Matched-pair case-control (each case matched to one control): build a paired table and use ONLY the discordant pairs: OR = (pairs where the case was exposed and the control was not)/(pairs where the control was exposed and the case was not) = b/c (e.g. 14 and 11 give OR = 1.27; 27 and 12 give 2.25). Do NOT use ad/bc on matched data. McNemar chi-square = (b - c)^2/(b + c), df 1 (e.g. (14-11)^2/25 = 0.36, not significant). Mantel-Haenszel (stratified) OR = sum over strata of (a_i d_i / n_i) divided by sum of (b_i c_i / n_i); it adjusts for a confounder (age group) by making one 2x2 per stratum and pooling; if stratum ORs differ a lot, that is effect modification. Odds from a probability: odds = p/(1-p); p = odds/(1+odds). Prevalence ratio (cross-sectional) = prevalence in exposed / prevalence in unexposed. Rate ratio/rate difference use person-time rates the same way.

WHICH MEASURE? (decision table). Cohort / outbreak among a known guest list / clinical trial / "attack rate" or "risk" asked: RR (and AR). Case-control / "cases and controls" / matched: OR. Cross-sectional survey: prevalence (ratio or OR). Ecological: no individual measures. Only exposed eaters listed: attack rate. Test result vs true disease: sensitivity/specificity. Counts over time in a population: incidence/prevalence. A trial of a vaccine or drug: RR, VE = 1 - RR, ARR, NNT. If the question says "state the measure and why", name it AND justify it with the design.

4. SENSITIVITY, SPECIFICITY, PPV, NPV (screening and diagnostic tests). Build the table with TRUE disease status (from the gold standard) in COLUMNS and TEST result in ROWS: TP = test+ and sick; FP = test+ and healthy; FN = test- and sick; TN = test- and healthy. Some tests flip it (test in columns) - check the labels. FORMULAS: Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN) = of the truly SICK people, the percent the test catches (divide by the SICK column). Specificity = TN/(TN+FP) = of the truly HEALTHY people, the percent the test clears (divide by the HEALTHY column). PPV = TP/(TP+FP) = of all POSITIVE tests, percent truly sick (divide by the test+ row). NPV = TN/(TN+FN) = of all NEGATIVE tests, percent truly healthy (test- row). Accuracy = (TP+TN)/total. Prevalence = (TP+FN)/total. False-positive rate = FP/(FP+TN) = 1 - specificity (specificity is NOT the false-positive rate). False-negative rate = 1 - sensitivity. LR+ = sensitivity/(1 - specificity); LR- = (1 - sensitivity)/specificity. Memory: SnNout (high Sensitivity, Negative result rules OUT) and SpPin (high Specificity, Positive rules IN). Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test and do NOT change with prevalence; PPV and NPV DO change: higher prevalence raises PPV and lowers NPV; rare disease gives low PPV even for a good test. Example: TP 135, FP 90, FN 16, TN 532 (N = 773): sensitivity 135/151 = 89.40%; specificity 532/622 = 85.53%; PPV 135/225 = 60.00%; NPV 532/548 = 97.08%; accuracy 667/773 = 86.29%. When only sensitivity, specificity, prevalence and N are given: (1) sick = N x prevalence; healthy = N - sick; (2) TP = sens x sick, FN = sick - TP; (3) TN = spec x healthy, FP = healthy - TN; (4) compute PPV, NPV. Example: sens 95%, spec 95%, prevalence 1%, N = 10,000: sick 100, TP 95, FN 5; healthy 9,900, TN 9,405, FP 495; PPV = 95/(95+495) = 16.1%, NPV = 9,405/9,410 = 99.95% (a positive result is wrong 5 times in 6 because the disease is rare). Bayes form: PPV = sens x prev / [sens x prev + (1-spec)(1-prev)]. Choosing: screening/outbreak (do not miss cases) wants HIGH SENSITIVITY (and NPV), then confirm positives with a specific test; confirming a diagnosis before drastic treatment wants HIGH SPECIFICITY. A broad (sensitive) case definition early in an outbreak; narrow (specific) later. Two tests: in SERIES (both must be positive) specificity and PPV go UP, sensitivity DOWN; in PARALLEL (either positive) sensitivity and NPV go UP, specificity DOWN. Back-solving a missing cell: use the formula that includes it (FP = TP x (1-PPV)/PPV; FN = TP x (1-sens)/sens; TP = PPV x number positive).

5. INCIDENCE, PREVALENCE AND RATES. Incidence proportion (cumulative incidence, risk) = NEW cases in a period / people AT RISK at the start (disease-free people), times 10^n. Incidence rate (incidence density) = new cases / total person-time at risk (e.g. per 1,000 person-years). Point prevalence = ALL existing cases (old + new) at one moment / population at that moment. Period prevalence = all people who had the disease at ANY time in the period / population. Prevalence = incidence x average duration (P = I x D), with I and D in the same time unit (incidence 1.65 per 100,000 per YEAR and 18 months = 1.5 YEARS: P = 1.65 x 1.5 = 2.475 per 100,000). High incidence + low prevalence = short or rapidly fatal illness; low incidence + high prevalence = chronic/long. Prevalence rises when survival lengthens or cure rate falls. STEPS for a "per N" rate: cases / population, then multiply by N (city 200,000, 500 new cases a year: 500/200,000 x 100,000 = 250 per 100,000 per year). Prevalence 1,200 cases / 200,000 = 6 per 1,000. Count from a rate: 101.9 per 100,000 x 39,500,000 people = 101.9 x 395 = 40,250.5 cases. Percent change = (new - old)/old x 100 ((150 - 68)/68 = 120.6% increase). Person-time: add up each person's years while disease-free; someone who becomes a case or is lost to follow-up stops counting (use half a year of exposure for that year ONLY if the test says so). Example: 7 new cases over 3,192.5 person-years = 0.002193 = 2.19 per 1,000 person-years. Person-time (rate) vs proportion: a proportion's numerator is part of its denominator (attack rate, CFR, incidence proportion); a ratio is a/b with separate groups (death-to-case ratio, male:female); a rate has time in the denominator. "Per 1000" vs "per 100,000": a count/population = 0.00025 = 25 per 100,000 = 0.25 per 1,000. Incidence uses people at risk (some tests accept the whole population; if "at risk" is stated, remove existing cases). Diagram questions (horizontal lines for patients over a month): incidence = lines that START in the period; point prevalence = lines present at the instant; period prevalence = every line touching the period. Apparent rise with no real change: better reporting, new test, publicity, changed case definition, bigger denominator, lab error, new notifiable status.

6. MORTALITY AND OTHER RATES. Case fatality rate (CFR) = deaths FROM the disease / people WITH the disease (cases) x 100 (10 deaths of 68 cases = 14.7%). Mortality (death) rate = deaths / WHOLE population (per 1,000 or 100,000); crude death rate = all deaths / midyear population; cause-specific mortality = deaths from the cause / population; age-(sex-)specific rate = deaths in that age (sex) group / population of the same group (340 deaths / 20,000 = 17 per 1,000); proportionate mortality = deaths from the cause / ALL deaths (20 of 200 = 10%); infant mortality rate = deaths under age 1 / live births x 1,000; neonatal = under 28 days / live births (35/2,600 = 13.46 per 1,000); crude birth rate = births / midyear population x 1,000; fertility rate = live births / women aged 15-44 x 1,000; death-to-case ratio = deaths/cases. Infectivity = infected / exposed; pathogenicity = clinically ill / infected; virulence = severe or dead / clinically ill (so a germ that infects many, makes few ill and few severely ill = infectivity HIGH, pathogenicity LOW, virulence LOW). Standardized (age-adjusted) rates remove the effect of different age structures when comparing populations: DIRECT = apply each age group's rate in the study population to a STANDARD population: adjusted rate = sum(rate_i x standard pop_i) / total standard pop (rates 60, 300, 750 per 100,000 in groups with standard populations 600k, 300k, 100k give expected 360 + 900 + 750 = 2,010 per 1,000,000 = 201.0 per 100,000; the crude rate could be very different). INDIRECT = apply standard rates to the study population to get EXPECTED deaths, then SMR = observed deaths / expected deaths (x100): 270/285 = 0.95; SMR 149 = 49% more deaths than expected; SMR 1155 = about 12 times as many. Use direct when your age-specific rates are reliable; indirect for small numbers (occupational cohorts). If two crude rates are equal but the age-adjusted rate is higher in A, A has the YOUNGER population. Years of potential life lost (YPLL) = sum over deaths below a chosen age of (life expectancy - age at death): LE 78, deaths at 30, 60, 85 = 48 + 18 + 0 = 66. DALY = YLL + YLD (Disability-ADJUSTED Life Years: years of life lost to early death plus years lived with disability; NOT "daily"). Doubling time = 70 / (growth rate in %) (2% a year = 35 years). Population pyramid: wide base = high birth rate, young; modal group = widest bar; median group = where the cumulative total reaches half.

7. VACCINE / TREATMENT EFFECT. Vaccine efficacy (trial, ideal conditions) or effectiveness (real world) VE = (AR unvaccinated - AR vaccinated)/AR unvaccinated = 1 - RR (x100). Steps: AR vaccinated, AR unvaccinated, divide, subtract from 1. Examples: 30/10,000 = 0.30% vs 80/10,000 = 0.80%: RR 0.375, VE = 62.5%. 5% vs 10%: VE 50%. 2% vs 20%: 90%. Incidence 2 vs 10 per 1,000 person-years: RR 0.2, VE 80%. Same as relative risk reduction (RRR) = (control risk - treated risk)/control risk. Absolute risk reduction ARR = control risk - treated risk (in percentage points; 9.0% - 6.0% = 3.0 points); NNT = 1/ARR (1/0.03 = 33.3; round UP to 34 people to treat to prevent one case). Attributable risk / risk increase (harm) = risk in exposed - risk in unexposed (3.0% vs 1.0% = 0.020); NNH = 1/attributable risk (1/0.02 = 50). Cost to prevent one case = NNT x cost per patient (33.3 x $1,000 = $33,333; compare with savings per case). A big RRR can be a tiny ARR when baseline risk is low, so always give baseline risk. Efficacy = ideal trial conditions; effectiveness = real-world observational data (US flu VE networks = effectiveness). Efficiency = outputs per resources used.

8. R0, HERD IMMUNITY AND TRANSMISSION MODELS. R0 (basic reproduction number) = average number of secondary cases one infectious person causes in a fully susceptible population; R0 = (contacts per time) x (probability of transmission per contact) x (duration infectious) (R0 2.87 with 3 contacts/day and 8 days: p = 2.87/(3 x 8) = 0.1196 = 12%). R0 < 1 outbreak dies out; = 1 steady/endemic; > 1 grows. Halving contacts halves R0 (12 to 6). R0 depends on the pathogen AND population/behavior but NOT on case fatality. Herd immunity threshold HIT = 1 - 1/R0 = (R0 - 1)/R0 (R0 = 5: 1 - 0.2 = 80%; measles R0 15: 93.3%; R0 3: 66.7%; R0 1.5: 33.3%; R0 2.37: 57.81%; R0 24: 95.83%). Steps: 1/R0, subtract from 1, x100. People to vaccinate = HIT x population, round UP (34,000 people, R0 3.5: 0.7143 x 34,000 = 24,286). Imperfect vaccine: coverage needed = HIT / vaccine efficacy (R0 3 gives HIT 66.7%; efficacy 75% needs 0.667/0.75 = 88.9% coverage; if the result exceeds 100% herd immunity from vaccination alone is impossible, e.g. 91.7%/0.90 = 101.9%). Check an option by coverage x efficacy >= HIT (0.95 x 0.75 = 0.7125 < 0.75 fails). Effective reproduction number Re (or R) = R0 x fraction still susceptible (R0 10, 85% susceptible: Re = 8.5; R0 12 with 88% immune: Re = 12 x 0.12 = 1.44; to reach Re = 1 need immune fraction 1 - 1/R0); next-generation cases = Re x current cases (Re 1.1 x 150 = 165). Two groups with different contact rates: vaccinate the high-contact group first. Epidemic spreads only if the susceptible fraction > 1/R0; at equilibrium R0 x S = 1. Number of generations: each case infects R others, one generation per serial interval: 2 infected, R = 2, 1 day per generation: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 > 24 classmates, so 4 days; "minimum whole R so that 24 infected reach 8,600 in 6 days": 24 x R^6 >= 8,600 so R >= 2.67, use 3. SIR model (S susceptible, I infected, R recovered; constant population, well mixed, permanent immunity, no births/deaths): counts form S' = -a x S x I, I' = a x S x I - b x I, R' = b x I (a = transmission coefficient, b = recovery rate; duration of infection = 1/b); proportion form s' = -beta s i, i' = beta s i - nu i, r' = nu i; R0 = beta/nu = (contact rate x transmissibility x duration). EULER STEPS (one day/week at a time): new S = S + S', new I = I + I', new R = R + R'. Example a = 0.0005, b = 0.125, S = 9,700, I = 300, R = 0: new infections = 0.0005 x 9,700 x 300 = 1,455; S' = -1,455; recoveries = 0.125 x 300 = 37.5; I' = 1,455 - 37.5 = 1,417.5; R' = 37.5; next day S = 8,245, I = 1,717.5, R = 37.5 (if told "round to integers" round each value as you go and reuse the rounded values); duration = 1/0.125 = 8 days. At the start of an epidemic S falls, I rises, R rises; mask mandates lower transmissibility (beta); SEIR adds an Exposed (latent) stage (sigma = 1/incubation rate); SEIRS adds waning immunity (waves). Higher R0 gives a higher AND earlier epidemic peak.

9. CHI-SQUARE TEST OF ASSOCIATION (2x2) - full procedure. Question: is the exposure-illness link bigger than chance? (1) State H0 (no association; exposure and illness independent) and H1 (association). (2) Write the observed 2x2 with row and column totals. (3) EXPECTED count for each cell = (row total x column total)/grand total (the expected for cell a is (a+b)(a+c)/N). (4) For each of the 4 cells compute (O - E)^2 / E and ADD them: chi-square = sum (O-E)^2/E. (5) df = (rows - 1)(columns - 1) = 1 for a 2x2. (6) Compare with the critical value: 3.84 at p = 0.05, 6.63 at 0.01, 10.83 at 0.001 (df 1); bigger = reject H0 = statistically significant. Example (bear meat, ate: 54 sick, 7 well; did not eat: 14 sick, 32 well; N = 107): sick total 68, well 39, ate 61, not 46. Expected: a = 61x68/107 = 38.77; b = 61x39/107 = 22.23; c = 46x68/107 = 29.23; d = 46x39/107 = 16.77. chi-square = 15.23^2/38.77 + 15.23^2/22.23 + 15.23^2/29.23 + 15.23^2/16.77 = 5.98 + 10.43 + 7.94 + 13.83 = 38.2 > 3.84: reject H0 (p < 0.001). Shortcut for 2x2: chi-square = N(ad - bc)^2 / [(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)]. Yates continuity correction lowers the value (use only if told). Chi-square gives significance, NOT strength or direction (a "significant" protective OR means the exposure is NOT the cause). Use FISHER'S EXACT test when any expected count is below 5 (small outbreaks); McNEMAR for matched pairs; Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (chi-square, df 1) for stratified 2x2 tables controlling a confounder; chi-square goodness-of-fit compares observed with expected counts in one variable; ANOVA compares 3+ means; t test compares 2 means; paired t test = same subjects measured twice; z test for large samples/known SD or for comparing proportions. Correlation r (Pearson): strength and direction of a LINEAR relationship, from -1 to +1; Cohen's kappa = (observed agreement - agreement expected by chance)/(1 - chance agreement), 0 to 1.

10. DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS. Mean = sum/n. Median = middle of the SORTED list (position (n+1)/2; if n is even average the two middle values: 10, 14, 18, 28, 34, 46, 54, 60, 72, 86 has median (34+46)/2 = 40, mean 42.2). Mode = most frequent value (can be several, or "none" if all unique). Range = max - min. Quartiles by median of halves: sorted 2, 10, 17, 20, 37, 40, 50: median 20, Q1 = median of (2, 10, 17) = 10, Q3 = median of (37, 40, 50) = 40; five-number summary 2, 10, 20, 40, 50; IQR = Q3 - Q1. Outliers drag the MEAN, not the median or IQR (median 1.5 vs mean 20.8 when one value is 360). Skew: right-skewed means mean > median. Choosing: nominal (sex, blood type) -> mode; ordinal -> median; skewed/outliers -> median; ratio/titers/exponential growth -> geometric mean = (product of n values)^(1/n) (2, 4, 8, 16, 32: product 32,768, fifth root = 8). Variance and SD, step by step: (1) mean; (2) each value minus the mean; (3) square each; (4) add them; (5) divide by n - 1 for a SAMPLE (n for a whole population - use sample unless told "population"); (6) SD = square root of variance. Example 13, 15, 4, 4, 1: mean 7.4; squared deviations 31.36, 57.76, 11.56, 11.56, 40.96 sum 153.2; sample variance 153.2/4 = 38.3; sample SD = 6.19 (population: 30.64 and 5.54). SD is in the original units; variance is in squared units; SD is NOT resistant to outliers; range, variance and SD all respond to extremes. z-score = (value - mean)/SD (91 when mean 82.65, SD 2.40: z = 3.48, very unusual; |z| > 3 is an outlier). Empirical rule (normal curve): 68% within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD (1.96), 99.7% within 3 SD (mean 60, SD 15: 45 to 75 is within 1 SD = 68%). Binomial probability of exactly k successes in n tries = C(n,k) p^k (1-p)^(n-k); geometric (first success on try k) = (1-p)^(k-1) p (p = 0.07, k = 5: 0.93^4 x 0.07 = 0.0524). Variable types: nominal, ordinal, interval (no true zero: temperature F/C, dates), ratio (true zero: weight, counts, time); discrete (counts) vs continuous. Percent of a stratum = part/whole ((37+76)/204 = 55.4%).

11. CONFIDENCE INTERVALS, t AND p-VALUES. 95% CI for a mean = mean +/- z x SD/sqrt(n), z = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.05 (96%), 2.576 (99%); with n < 30 use the t value with df = n - 1 (t about 2.13 for n = 16, 2.26 for n = 10). SE = SD/sqrt(n). Example: mean 90, SD 6, n = 1,000, 96% CI: SE = 6/31.62 = 0.1897; margin 2.05 x 0.1897 = 0.389; CI = 89.6 to 90.4. CI for a proportion = p +/- 1.96 x sqrt(p(1-p)/n) (40 of 100: SE 0.049, CI 30.4% to 49.6%). One-sample t = (sample mean - hypothesized mean)/(SD/sqrt(n)), df n - 1 (mean 24.1, SD 5.6, n = 16, null 38.0: SE 1.4, t = -9.93, far beyond 2.13: significant). Paired t: subtract each pair, then do a one-sample t on the differences (mean difference/(SD of differences/sqrt(n)), df n - 1). Bland-Altman limits of agreement = mean difference +/- 1.96 x SD of differences. Reading a CI for an OR or RR: if the 95% interval does NOT contain 1 the result is significant (OR 3.5, CI 1.2-8.8: significant risk factor but wide = imprecise); if it contains 1 (0.8-4.5) it is NOT significant; "26% to 99% higher odds" for CI 1.26-1.99; a wider CI means less precision (smaller sample). Correct meaning: if the study were repeated many times, 95% of such intervals would contain the true value. p-value = probability of results at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true; p < 0.05 -> statistically significant -> reject H0; it does NOT prove cause, is not the probability H0 is true, and depends on sample size; "p < 0.01" is not "99% confident of the effect". Type I error (alpha) = rejecting a TRUE null (false positive); Type II error (beta) = failing to reject a FALSE null (false negative); power = 1 - beta (80% is enough); more subjects, a bigger effect, a larger alpha or smaller variance all raise power and shrink random error; a tiny difference needs a huge sample. Random error (precision) improves with sample size; systematic error (bias, accuracy) does not. Null hypothesis = no difference/no association/no effect (RR or OR = 1).

12. EPI-CURVE AND INCUBATION MATH. Incubation period = exposure to symptom onset (onset time minus exposure time; hours vs days - convert). Party Feb 13 at 7 pm, first onset Feb 14 at 2 pm = 19 hours; last onset Feb 21 at 9 am = 182 hours (7 days 14 h); the incubation RANGE is 19 to 182 hours. Median incubation = time from exposure to the PEAK of a point-source curve. Unknown exposure time (find the exposure window): window starts at (first case onset - MAXIMUM incubation) and ends at (last case onset - MINIMUM incubation) (first case Mar 27, last Apr 19, incubation 9-48 days: start Mar 27 - 48 d = Feb 7, end Apr 19 - 9 d = Apr 10, a 62-day window; ask what everyone did in that window). With the peak and a known median incubation: exposure = peak date - median incubation. Shapes: POINT SOURCE = one sharp peak within one incubation period (single exposure; wedding, picnic); CONTINUOUS common source = plateau; INTERMITTENT = irregular peaks; PROPAGATED = successive taller waves spaced about one incubation period apart (person-to-person), ending when susceptibles run out or control starts. Pick x-axis intervals about 1/4 to 1/3 of the incubation period. Index case = first case that comes to attention; primary = first infected; secondary = infected by a case. Outbreak is declared over after 2 x the maximum incubation period with no new cases (Ebola 21 d: 42 days). Declared sooner = too early. Late/early outliers = secondary cases, different exposure or unusual incubation.

13. WORDING TRAPS SEEN ON REAL TESTS. (a) "Attack rate" may be asked on case-control data (cases among eaters/all eaters) - compute as written, but the proper measure there is OR. (b) A stem that tells a story ("the water caused it") may have RR < 1 - answer what the numbers show. (c) "Rate" is often loosely used for proportions (cases/residents "per 1,000"). (d) Prevalence "during July" = period prevalence. (e) Table totals that do not add up - use cells. (f) Directions that conflict ("thousandths" vs "significant figures") - give the decimal asked and mention the other. (g) Show formula, substitution, units, interpretation. (h) Two 2x2 tables for 5-10 foods: compute all, compare. (i) PPV questions at 1% prevalence: expect a low answer. (j) A matched or paired design needs the matched formula. (k) HIT uses R0 only (not attack rate or OR). (l) RR and OR describe association, not proof of causation (needs Hill criteria, confounders ruled out).

FULL WORKED OUTBREAK (do this on any "find the culprit" table). A wedding: 100 guests interviewed, 60 ill. Chicken: ate 50 (45 ill, 5 well), did not eat 50 (15 ill, 35 well). Cake: ate 70 (42 ill, 28 well), did not 30 (18 ill, 12 well). Rice: ate 40 (28 ill, 12 well), did not 60 (32 ill, 28 well). STEP 1 design: a known guest list = retrospective cohort, so use attack rates and RR. STEP 2 attack rates (eaters vs non-eaters): chicken 90% vs 30%; cake 60% vs 60%; rice 70% vs 53.3%. STEP 3 RR: chicken 3.00; cake 1.00; rice 1.31. STEP 4 (optional) OR = ad/bc: chicken (45 x 35)/(5 x 15) = 21.0; cake 1.00; rice 2.04. STEP 5 chi-square (shortcut N(ad-bc)^2/[(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)]): chicken 100 x (1575 - 75)^2/(50 x 50 x 60 x 40) = 37.5 > 3.84, significant; cake 0; rice 2.78 < 3.84, not significant. STEP 6 coverage: 45 of 60 ill people (75%) ate the chicken. CONCLUSION: chicken is the most likely vehicle (highest AR among eaters, lowest among non-eaters, highest RR, significant, most ill people ate it); cake shows no association; rice is weak and not significant. NEXT: confirm with the lab (stool cultures, leftover chicken), check the cooking and holding steps, draw the epi curve (point source if one sharp peak), exclude ill food handlers, recall/discard, communicate findings. ANSWER TEMPLATE for free response: name the measure and why (design), write the formula, substitute, give the number with units, say what it means in plain words, state the conclusion and caveat (association is not proof; check confounders, bias, and the Hill criteria).

WHAT EPIDEMIOLOGY IS. Epidemiology = the study of the DISTRIBUTION (who, where, when - descriptive) and DETERMINANTS (causes and risk factors - analytic) of health-related states or events in specified POPULATIONS, and the APPLICATION of that knowledge to control disease (focus is groups, not diagnosing/treating one patient). Reasons to investigate outbreaks: control and prevention, research/learning, training, public/political/legal concern, program considerations. Types: CLASSICAL (distribution and determinants in populations), CLINICAL (applies it to prevention, detection, treatment in clinical settings), INFECTIOUS disease (needs lab support), CHRONIC disease (complex sampling and statistics), DESCRIPTIVE (person, place, time), ANALYTIC (case-control or cohort, hypothesis testing, causes and effects; hallmark = a comparison group), OCCUPATIONAL (workers exposed to chemical, biological, physical agents). Clinical approach = individual patient, diagnosis and treatment (giving one patient a vaccine; taking blood pressure; prescribing); public health approach = whole population, prevention and policy (smoking ban, recall of lettuce, quarantine, immunization campaign). Public health functions: assessment (find and describe problems), policy development, assurance (carry out and evaluate programs). First step in problem solving: define the problem.

KEY VOCABULARY. Etiology = study of cause. Determinant = any factor (event, characteristic) that changes health; health determinants: genes/biology, health behaviors, social/total ecology (largest), health services. Index case = first case that comes to the attention of health authorities (patient zero as documented); primary case = first person actually infected who brought it in; secondary case = infected by a case. Attrition = people leaving a study (death, disinterest, moving; causes selection bias). Correlation does not equal causation. Confounder = outside variable associated with BOTH exposure and outcome and NOT on the causal pathway, making a false (or hiding a real) association; control by randomization, restriction, matching (design) or stratification, standardization, regression, Mantel-Haenszel (analysis). Effect modification = true effect differs by stratum. Simpson's paradox = crude and stratified results disagree. Infectious = caused by an organism; contagious = spreads person to person; noninfectious (noncommunicable) = genetics, defects, aging, environment (cancer, heart disease, diabetes). Morbidity = illness; mortality = death; natality = births. Risk = probability an individual develops disease in a stated time. Demographic transition = shift from high birth and death rates to low; epidemiologic transition (Omran) = infectious to chronic disease. Preventable risk factors: alcohol, smoking, diet, inactivity, poor sleep; non-preventable: age, genetics, sex, family history, ethnicity.

TRIADS AND CHAIN. EPIDEMIOLOGIC TRIAD = AGENT (what causes it: virus, bacterium, chemical) + HOST (organism the agent infects or harms) + ENVIRONMENT (what brings agent and host together; includes modes of transmission); a vector is not a separate triad corner and "behavior" is not a corner. DESCRIPTIVE triad = PERSON, PLACE, TIME. CHAIN OF INFECTION (6 links, break any one): infectious agent -> reservoir (where it lives and multiplies: humans, animals, environment) -> portal of exit (respiratory tract, GI/feces/saliva, skin/wounds, blood/needles, genitals, placenta) -> mode of transmission -> portal of entry (mouth, nose, cuts, eyes, genitals, bite) -> susceptible host (immunocompromised, unvaccinated, young, old). Chain of transmission (short form): external agent, carrier (fomite or vector), vulnerable host. Break it at the reservoir (treat, isolate, cull), at transmission (handwashing, masks, safe food and water, bed nets, sterile needles), or at the host (vaccine, prophylaxis). Example TB: reservoir infected human; exit respiratory tract (cough); airborne droplet nuclei; entry respiratory tract; susceptible host.

TYPES OF OCCURRENCE (-EMICS). Cluster = aggregation of cases closely grouped in time and place (does not require "more than expected" and is the smallest event). Outbreak = more cases than expected in a given area and time (usually local; 40 salmonella cases from one meal). Epidemic = rapid spread to many hosts in a larger area/short time (200 Marburg cases in several districts). Pandemic = epidemic over multiple countries/continents (Black Death 1346-1353, 1918 flu, COVID-19). Sporadic = infrequent, irregular, no pattern. Endemic = constant usual presence in an area (the baseline; malaria in parts of Africa; common cold). Hyperendemic = persistently high in ALL age groups (also used for a persistently high rate); holoendemic = high prevalence in children year-round, adults largely immune (malaria); hypoendemic = low incidence, small affected population; syndemic = 2+ epidemics interact and worsen each other (obesity + diabetes + heart disease; Lyme + co-infections; synergistic). Endemic diseases can still have outbreaks. Plague = life-threatening flea-borne disease (Yersinia pestis) with three forms: bubonic (most common; swollen buboes), septicemic (blood), pneumonic (lungs; spread by droplets).

DISEASE TRANSMISSION - FULL LIST. Nosocomial = acquired in a hospital (not always; a patient may arrive incubating); zoonosis = animal to human (anthroponosis = human to animal; reverse zoonosis); iatrogenic = caused by medical treatment or procedure. DIRECT (person to person or soil): skin-to-skin, sex, kissing, bites/rabies (mononucleosis, gonorrhea, flu, hookworm from soil); DROPLET spread = large droplets (> 5 micrometres) from cough/sneeze/talk reach a susceptible person within about 1 meter before touching the ground (pertussis, meningococcal disease, flu; CDC counts droplet as direct); VERTICAL = mother to baby (transplacental, perinatal, breast milk; HIV, rubella, Zika, syphilis); VENEREAL = sex; FECAL-ORAL = fecal particles reach another mouth through poor hygiene or sanitation. INDIRECT: AIRBORNE = droplet nuclei or dust (< 5 micrometres) stay suspended and travel more than 1 meter, ventilation matters (measles, TB, COVID); VEHICLEBORNE = contaminated food, water, blood, biological products (hepatitis A, botulism, salmonella; botulinum grows in improperly canned food); FOMITES = inanimate objects (scalpels, handkerchiefs, bedding, doorknobs, towels, syringes; the comb is the fomite and the lice are the agent); VECTORBORNE = arthropods: MECHANICAL vector carries the agent on its body without change (flies carrying Shigella, fleas carrying Yersinia), BIOLOGICAL vector = agent multiplies or develops in the vector (malaria in Anopheles, Lyme in Ixodes ticks, guinea worm in copepods); WATERBORNE = fecally contaminated water (cholera, dysentery, E. coli). Vector = living animal that carries the agent but does not itself cause the disease; vehicle = non-living carrier. CARRIERS: incubatory (infectious before symptoms), convalescent (infectious after recovery, often thinking themselves cured), transient/temporary (short time), chronic (long time; Typhoid Mary, Salmonella Typhi, Mary Mallon: Irish cook, 53 cases), genetic (carries an inherited trait without symptoms), passive (mechanical carrier). A symptom-free person can still be infectious.

AGENT CHARACTERISTICS (use the right denominator). INFECTIVITY = ability to cause infection = exposed people who become infected / exposed = a/(a+b). PATHOGENICITY = infected people who develop clinical (apparent) disease / infected. VIRULENCE = severity: apparent cases that are severe or fatal / apparent cases (morbidity and mortality). Iceberg phenomenon = only a small proportion of infections are clinically apparent. Spectrum of disease = full range from subclinical through mild, severe, disability, to death.

STAGES OF DISEASE (natural history). SUSCEPTIBILITY (host exposed to enough factors to start the process; infectious microorganisms, asbestos, smoking) -> SUBCLINICAL (asymptomatic, pathologic changes without the host knowing; DORMANT = agent can be recovered, LATENT = agent cannot be recovered directly) -> CLINICAL/illness (symptoms visible; highly communicable) -> RECOVERY, DISABILITY or DEATH (decline, sometimes convalescence). INCUBATION PERIOD = exposure to first symptoms (acute infection, agent replicating). LATENT PERIOD = exposure to infectiousness; in chronic disease "latency" = exposure to symptoms (Hiroshima exposure to leukemia years later). PRODROMAL = early warning signs before obvious illness (fastigium = peak of fever/symptoms; defervescence = fever falling). Pathogenesis steps: exposure, adhesion, invasion, infection (or incubation, prodromal, illness, convalescence). Infection stages: incubation, prodromal, illness, decline, convalescence. A sign is a disease indicator a clinician observes; a symptom is felt by the patient. Prophylaxis = action to prevent disease; fulminant = sudden, severe onset.

CASE DEFINITION AND SURVEILLANCE. A case definition has 4 parts: CLINICAL information (symptoms, lab confirmation), PERSON (age, sex, race, occupation), PLACE (geographic or facility), TIME (specific period). Example: "any person with vomiting or diarrhea who attended the event on [date]". Levels: SUSPECTED/POSSIBLE (symptoms only), PROBABLE (compatible illness plus epidemiologic link, no lab), CONFIRMED (lab-confirmed); not a case. Early in an outbreak use a broad, SENSITIVE definition; later a narrow, specific one. CDC food-outbreak steps: detect (monitor reports), find (case definition and look for more cases), generate hypotheses (interview food histories), test (compare people with and without the exposure), solve (confirm the contaminated food and point of contamination), control (recall, sanitation, advice), decide (outbreak over when no new illness and no contaminated food). SURVEILLANCE = systematic, ongoing collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination of health data in order to prevent and control. Five steps: identify/define/measure the health problem; collect data; analyze and interpret; provide data and interpretation to health authorities (disseminate); monitor and evaluate the system (and take action). Alternate version: data collection, analysis, interpretation, distribution, action (surveillance alone does not always include control actions). PASSIVE (providers and labs report; cheap, wide, but underreported and incomplete: notifiable-disease reports, lab reporting, registries [complete but slow, expensive], VAERS). ACTIVE (health department contacts providers or does surveys; complete, accurate, expensive; sentinel physician networks, serial surveys). SENTINEL (a chosen network or subset of providers reports; good trends, not representative, poor for rare diseases). SYNDROMIC (early symptoms or proxies: school absenteeism, ER chief complaints, drug sales; timely early warning, low specificity). Notifiable disease = CDC/CSTE list (reported to state health departments; botulism and lead are notifiable to find a common source); reportable = must be reported to local or state health departments. Surveillance attributes: timeliness, sensitivity, representativeness, simplicity, flexibility, acceptability, predictive value positive. Data sources: vital records, notifiable disease reports, hospital data, surveys; RCTs are not surveillance. Agencies: CDC (founded July 1, 1946 as the Communicable Disease Center, Atlanta; Joseph Mountin; HQ Atlanta, part of HHS), WHO (UN health agency, HQ Geneva; promotes health, security, systems, evidence), MMWR (CDC weekly), NNDSS (national notifiable disease system), FoodNet (active foodborne surveillance), NORS, NARMS, FluSurv-NET, NREVSS, CaliciNet (norovirus), PulseNet (below). Reasons incidence may rise falsely: better reporting system, new test, publicity, case definition change, lab error, false positives, population change, newly notifiable.

OUTBREAK INVESTIGATION - THE STEPS. CDC 10-step list (the one most tests use): 1 prepare for field work; 2 establish the existence of an outbreak (compare observed with expected baseline); 3 verify the diagnosis (lab and clinical); 4 construct a case definition and identify/find cases systematically (line list); 5 describe and orient the data by time, place and person (epi curve, spot map); 6 develop hypotheses (agent, host, environment); 7 evaluate hypotheses epidemiologically (compare ill with well using a cohort or case-control study; needs a control group); 8 refine hypotheses and carry out additional studies, comparing and reconciling with lab and environmental findings; 9 implement control and prevention measures (as soon as possible, often earlier); 10 communicate findings. 13-step version (old notes, also tested; steps done simultaneously): 1 prepare for field work; 2 establish existence, severity, potential for spread, public concern, resources; 3 verify diagnosis; 4 construct a working case definition (can combine with 5); 5 find cases systematically and record information; 6 describe and orient the data by person, place, time; 7 develop hypotheses (agent/host/environment + chain of transmission); 8 evaluate hypotheses (MUST have a control group); 9 refine hypotheses if necessary; 10 compare and reconcile with lab and/or environmental studies (can combine with 9); 11 implement control and prevention measures (ASAP); 12 initiate or maintain surveillance (can combine with 11); 13 communicate findings. A line listing = spreadsheet with one row per case and columns for variables (person, place, time, symptoms, lab). Early interviews generate hypotheses; a hypothesis is tested with a comparison group; use a cohort study when the population is known (wedding guests, team) and a case-control when it is large or undefined. Outbreak graphs: spot map shows where cases live or were exposed (Snow); an area (choropleth) map must show rates not counts and hides variation inside areas; epi curve = histogram of cases by onset time (types in the math section: point source, continuous, intermittent, propagated); line chart, bar graph, scatter plot also used. Age pattern of deaths: seasonal flu U-shaped (infants and elderly), 1918 flu W-shaped (young adults died too).

FOODBORNE ILLNESS. FATTOM (conditions pathogens need): Food (protein-rich), Acidity (pH 6.6-7.5 best; FDA requires below 4.5 to stop growth), Time (do not leave food in the danger zone more than 2-4 hours), Temperature (danger zone 41-135 F; poultry 165 F, ground meat 160 F), Oxygen (most are aerobic; C. botulinum is anaerobic and grows in low-acid canned food), Moisture (water activity aw 0-1.0; pathogens thrive at 0.95-1.0; FDA requires below 0.85). Preservation by salt, sugar, drying, freezing, acid works by cutting water or lowering pH. Prevention: clean, separate (avoid cross-contamination), cook, chill; cook meat, poultry and eggs thoroughly; refrigerate leftovers; wash produce; report suspected foodborne illness to the health department. Foodborne INTOXICATION = preformed toxin (S. aureus in potato or egg salad, B. cereus, C. perfringens), fast onset 30 min-8 h, no fever, live bacteria not needed, antibiotics useless; foodborne INFECTION = live organisms multiply in gut (Salmonella, Campylobacter, E. coli, Listeria). Top US foodborne germs: norovirus (most common cause of foodborne illness; low infectious dose; vomiting, diarrhea; incubation 12-48 h, median about 33 h; peaks in winter; spread by food handlers, shellfish, produce, surfaces; control with soap handwashing, bleach, excluding sick food workers 48 h after symptoms; no vaccine; diagnosed by RT-PCR), Salmonella, C. perfringens, Campylobacter, S. aureus. Food-pathogen matches: botulism = home-canned vegetables; Listeria = deli meats, hot dogs, soft cheese, unpasteurized dairy (survives refrigeration; dangerous in pregnancy); E. coli O157:H7/STEC = undercooked ground beef, produce (Shiga toxin, hemolytic uremic syndrome; do NOT give antibiotics); Vibrio vulnificus/parahaemolyticus = raw shellfish, seawater; cyclospora = fresh produce; Salmonella = eggs, poultry, raw milk, reptiles, turtles (4-inch law 1975), backyard poultry; Campylobacter = poultry, raw milk, undercooked chicken liver; hepatitis A = frozen strawberries (1997), green onions; cholera = water; Cryptosporidium = water (Milwaukee 1993). Known outbreaks: Jack in the Box 1993 E. coli O157:H7; Blue Bell 2015 Listeria; Iowa eggs 2010 Salmonella Enteritidis; Chipotle 2015 E. coli. PulseNet = CDC network of labs that compares the DNA fingerprints of bacteria (PFGE band patterns, now whole-genome sequencing) to link cases across states; works for bacteria, not for viruses or prions. Pasteurization = brief heat; FSIS (USDA) regulates meat, poultry, eggs; FDA the rest; HACCP controls hazards, FSMA/HARPC emphasizes prevention. Safe-handling basics: thaw in refrigerator, cold water or microwave only.

ENVIRONMENTAL HEALTH. Environmental quality = biological, chemical and physical causes of illness (pollution, smoking, disasters, toxins, pesticides); WHO: about 24% of disease is caused by environmental factors. AIR: ground-level ozone (asthma, poor lung function, premature death) comes from precursor chemicals, methane, wildfires, stagnation; particle pollution = dust, dirt, soot from primary sources (wood stove, which pollute directly) and secondary sources (cars, form particles from gases); air pollution causes COPD. Lead: old paint, leaded gasoline, pipes, batteries; blood lead threshold 10 ug/dL (now lower); lead lowers IQ. Pesticides and metals bioaccumulate (build up in one organism) and biomagnify (rise at each higher trophic level; long-lived predatory fish such as tuna and swordfish carry the most mercury; Minamata disease = methylmercury). Clean water: boiling, chlorination, filtration, UV; floods promote cholera; acute diarrhea treatment = oral rehydration + zinc; rotavirus is the leading global cause of diarrhea in children. Mosquito control: remove standing water, screens, bed nets with insecticide, repellents. Climate change widens mosquito and tick ranges. Motor vehicle safety: seat belts, airbags, child seats. Top CDC public health challenges: alcohol-related harms, food safety, healthcare-associated infections, heart disease/stroke, HIV, motor vehicle injuries, nutrition/physical activity/obesity, prescription drug overdose, teen pregnancy, tobacco. Leading cause of death in the US: heart disease.

IMMUNITY AND VACCINES. ACTIVE immunity: exposure to a live pathogen or vaccine; own immune response, memory cells (long-lasting). PASSIVE immunity: antibodies made elsewhere, short term (gamma globulin, antitoxin/antivenom, maternal antibodies crossing the placenta or in milk = natural passive). ARTIFICIAL immunity = deliberate exposure (vaccine). HERD IMMUNITY: when enough people are immune the chain breaks and even the unvaccinated (infants, immunocompromised) are indirectly protected; threshold HIT = (R0 - 1)/R0 (higher R0 needs more people immune; pockets of low coverage allow local outbreaks). HIV kills helper T (CD4) cells; U=U: HIV undetectable for 6 months cannot be transmitted sexually; HIV is the virus, AIDS the late stage. Inactivated (killed) vs live attenuated vaccines; toxoid; conjugate; mRNA (cold chain, rare myocarditis). Antigenic drift = small point mutations (seasonal flu, yearly vaccine, partial immunity); antigenic shift = major reassortment (pandemic strain). Antigen = foreign substance that triggers an immune response; antibody = blood protein that counters a specific antigen. Quarantine = restricts movement of well people who MAY have been exposed; isolation = separates SICK people. Contact tracing = find and monitor exposed contacts. Control vocabulary: control (reduce to an acceptable level, needs continued effort), elimination of disease or infection, eradication (permanent worldwide zero; smallpox is the only human disease eradicated; polio wild virus remains in Afghanistan and Pakistan), extinction. Bacteriophage = virus that infects bacteria; virophage needs a co-infecting giant virus. Bioterrorism = deliberate release of live agents. Arbovirus = arthropod-borne virus (dengue, West Nile, chikungunya, Zika, yellow fever). Exogenous infection = from contact with a contaminated surface; endogenous = the pathogen already colonized the body (nose).

PREVENTION LEVELS (with examples tests use). PRIMORDIAL = stop risk factors from arising by changing social and physical environments (sidewalks, salt/sugar policy, fluoridation policy). PRIMARY = prevent the exposure or disease before it starts (vaccines, handwashing, chlorination, seat belts, helmets, health education, smoking bans). SECONDARY = detect early in the subclinical (latent) stage and treat to stop progression (screening, mammograms, post-exposure prophylaxis). TERTIARY = in the symptomatic stage, slow or reverse progression and limit disability (rehabilitation, preventing a second stroke, naloxone after overdose, treating complications). QUATERNARY = protect from unnecessary or excessive medical intervention (antibiotic stewardship, avoiding over-treatment; keeps doctors' social credit). Prevention before symptoms = primary + secondary. Screening works when the disease is serious, early treatment helps, the test is acceptable and accurate, prevalence is high enough, and follow-up care exists.

STUDY DESIGNS (pros and cons). EXPERIMENTAL: investigator assigns the exposure and tracks outcomes; RCT/clinical trial (human experimentation with controlled exposure, randomization, usually blinding; gold standard for causation; most scientifically sound and best control of exposure; cons: time-consuming, very expensive, unethical for harmful exposures, restricted generalizability, risk can cause disinterest); single-blind (subjects), double-blind (subjects and researchers), triple-blind (+ analysts); community trial = whole community is the study group; field trial; QUASI-experiment = no randomization (more generalizable, weaker causal conclusions). OBSERVATIONAL: investigators only observe. COHORT: follow exposed and unexposed people, see who gets disease, compare rates; gives incidence and RR, good for rare exposures and multiple outcomes, correct time sequence; cons: slow, expensive, attrition, bad for rare diseases; PROSPECTIVE (followed forward from before outcomes) or RETROSPECTIVE (past records; two groups identified after the fact; may lack confounder data). CASE-CONTROL: start with sick and healthy comparison people and look back for exposures; gives OR; cheap, fast, good for rare disease and long latency, multiple exposures; cons: recall bias, selection of controls, only one outcome, no incidence or RR, time order unclear; controls must come from the same at-risk source population. CROSS-SECTIONAL: everyone measured once for exposure and outcome (survey); gives prevalence; fastest, cheapest, multiple outcomes; cons: cannot separate incidence from survival, no temporality, least confident. ECOLOGICAL: groups, not individuals (ecological fallacy = applying a group association to individuals). NATURAL experiment: nature assigns exposure (Snow's water companies). Case report (one patient), case series (several, no comparison group). Descriptive designs: case reports, case series, cross-sectional, ecological. Hierarchy of evidence (low to high): case report < ecological < cross-sectional < case-control < cohort < RCT < systematic review/meta-analysis (meta-analysis combines independent studies). GRADE rates certainty of evidence; RCTs start high; it is moved DOWN for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias and UP for large effect and dose-response; strength of association and dose-response matter. PICO = Population, Intervention, Control, Outcome; FINER = feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant. Sequential testing = sample size not fixed in advance; boundaries narrow as data accumulate. Internal validity = valid within the study; external validity = generalizable; random selection improves generalizability, not internal validity.

CAUSATION. BRADFORD HILL (9): (1) Strength of association (large risk estimate); (2) Consistency (repeatable in different populations and times); (3) Specificity (one cause gives a specific effect); (4) TEMPORALITY (exposure before outcome - the only absolutely required one); (5) Biological gradient/dose-response (more exposure, more risk); (6) Plausibility (a biologically sensible mechanism); (7) Coherence (epidemiologic findings agree with laboratory/biological knowledge and past studies; a lab strain match of the food agent counts here or as experiment); (8) Experiment (altering the exposure alters the outcome); (9) ANALOGY (similar cause-effect elsewhere). The old notes listed "alternative explanations" in place of analogy; CDC/other lists of "six" use: temporal relationship, strength, dose-response, replication/consistency, biologic plausibility, consideration of alternative explanations. KOCH'S POSTULATES (4; Koch also studied anthrax and TB): the microbe is (1) abundant in all cases and absent in healthy organisms, (2) isolated from the diseased host and grown in pure culture, (3) the cultured microbe causes disease in a healthy host, (4) re-isolated from the inoculated host and identical to the original. Limits: healthy carriers (Typhoid Mary) break 1; viruses, prions and obligate intracellular bacteria (Chlamydia, Rickettsia) cannot be pure-cultured (2); non-infectious causes do not fit; "asymptomatic after treatment" is not a postulate. EVANS' POSTULATES (10, 1976; based on Koch and Hill plus host response): 1 disease prevalence is higher in the exposed; 2 exposure is more frequent in those with disease; 3 in prospective studies incidence is higher in the exposed; 4 disease follows exposure with a normal or log-normal distribution of incubation periods; 5 a spectrum of host responses from mild to severe follows exposure; 6 a measurable host response appears (or rises) after exposure; 7 in experiments disease occurs more in the exposed than controls; 8 reducing or removing the risk factor reduces disease; 9 modifying the host response reduces disease; 10 everything makes biological and epidemiological sense. ROTHMAN'S CAUSAL PIES: sufficient cause = the whole pie; necessary cause = appears in every pie; component cause = one slice. Web of causation; DAG (directed acyclic graph) maps causal and confounding paths. Efficiency = results per resources; efficacy (ideal conditions) vs effectiveness (real conditions). Hormesis = low-dose stimulation, high-dose inhibition. Reduce confounding by randomization, restriction, matching, then stratification and regression. Matching drawbacks: cannot study the matched variable, overmatching, matched analysis needed.

ERROR AND BIAS (names and definitions). Random error = chance fluctuation around the true value (cannot be corrected; shrinks with larger samples and more precise measurement); precision = inverse of random error; systematic error = consistent error (bias; not reduced by a larger sample; accuracy/validity). Selection bias: the chosen subjects are not representative or randomization fails (attrition, non-response, undercoverage such as landline-only surveys, healthy participant/volunteer bias, Berkson bias from hospital-based controls, referral bias, exclusion bias, Neyman/prevalence-incidence bias - prevalent cases miss early deaths and mild cases). Information/measurement bias: error in measurement or observation: RECALL bias (cases remember exposures differently; reduced by objective records or sick controls), INTERVIEWER bias (interviewer probes cases more; common in case-control), surrogate interviewer bias (next of kin remember only good things), family information bias, reporting bias, response/social-desirability bias, differential misclassification (unequal misclassification by exposure group; non-differential pushes toward no association), media/Baader-Meinhof bias. Surveillance/detection bias = a monitored group is checked more thoroughly. Temporal bias = unknown timeline of exposure and outcome. Lead-time bias = screening finds disease earlier so survival APPEARS longer; length-time bias = screening finds slow, less aggressive tumors that have longer preclinical phases. Protopathic bias = a drug is given for early undiagnosed disease. Confounding bias = mixing of effects. Hawthorne effect = people change behavior because they know they are watched; Pygmalion/Rosenthal effect = researcher expectations alter results; panel effect = repeated surveys of the same people. Publication bias; aggregation (ecological) bias. Healthy-worker effect = workers are healthier than the general population (which includes the disabled and unemployed), so occupational death rates look falsely low. Type 1 error = rejecting a TRUE null (false positive, alpha); Type 2 = failing to reject a FALSE null (false negative, beta); Type 3 = right answer to the wrong question; Type 4 = wrong question or analysis/misinterpretation. POWER = 1 - P(Type 2); 80% is enough for credibility.

RATE VOCABULARY SHORT LIST. Morbidity rate = new cases/population; mortality rate = deaths/population; birth rate = births per year/midyear population; fertility rate = live births per 1,000 women aged 15-44; death rate = deaths per year/midyear population; case fatality rate = deaths from disease/people with disease; cause-specific mortality = deaths from the disease/population; proportionate mortality = deaths from cause in the target group/all deaths. Risk of illness is treated as incidence ("x cases / y population / z time"). Incidence suits cohort studies and acute diseases; prevalence suits cross-sectional studies. Point prevalence = at one time; period prevalence = over a duration. Secondary attack rate = infections among susceptible close contacts (household), showing how social interaction affects transmission risk. EFFICACY old-notes confusion: vaccine effectiveness = 1 - (attack rate vaccinated/attack rate unvaccinated); it is NOT "new cases/total population x 1000" (that is incidence).

HISTORY AND PEOPLE (match the person to the deed). HIPPOCRATES (about 460-370 BC): father of modern medicine; first to explain disease rationally by environment and lifestyle; recorded disease in different populations. JOHN GRAUNT (1600s UK): father of demography and vital statistics; analyzed London's weekly Bills of Mortality (1662), used ratios and the rule of three to estimate population, birth and death rates, saw male/female differences, began statistical association and inference; doubted plague death reports; investigated 1634 deaths blamed on "rickets". THOMAS SYDENHAM (1624-89): recorded observations of disease apart from Hippocratic theory, separated fevers (continued, intermittent) and smallpox in London. JAMES LIND (1716-94): Scottish naval surgeon; experimental trial of 6 treatment pairs on sailors with scurvy; citrus cured them in 6 days (first controlled clinical trial); lime juice added to rations, sailors called "limeys". EDWARD JENNER (1749-1823): cowpox used to make the first smallpox vaccine (1796); Benjamin Jesty inoculated earlier. IGNAZ SEMMELWEIS (1800s): Vienna maternity clinic with high childbed (puerperal) fever death in the doctors' clinic; physicians moved from autopsies to pelvic exams; he required handwashing in chlorinated lime. JOHN SNOW (1813-58): anesthesiologist and founder of modern/field epidemiology; 1854 London cholera, Broad Street pump in Soho, spot map, compared water companies (natural experiment), removed the pump handle; rejected miasma. WILLIAM FARR (1807-83): founder of medical statistics, England's cause-of-death system, foundations of surveillance. LOUIS PASTEUR (1822-95): germ theory, disproved spontaneous generation, pasteurization, anthrax and rabies vaccines. ROBERT KOCH (1843-1910): postulates; anthrax, TB, cholera. PERCIVALL POTT: scrotal cancer in chimney sweeps (first occupational carcinogen). WALTER REED: yellow fever and mosquitoes. EDGAR SYDENSTRICKER: first morbidity study (Hagerstown, Maryland, 1921-24). JOSEPH GOLDBERGER: pellagra is a niacin (diet) deficiency. ALEXANDER FLEMING: accidentally discovered penicillin (1928). ALEXANDER LANGMUIR (1910-93): key to developing CDC, applied epidemiology and surveillance. JOSEPH MOUNTIN: founded the CDC (July 1, 1946; as the Communicable Disease Center, to fight malaria). MARY MALLON (Typhoid Mary, 1900s): Irish cook, healthy chronic carrier of Salmonella Typhi, responsible for about 50 (old notes: 53) cases of typhoid in about 15 years, 3 deaths; led to protection of public food supplies. FRAMINGHAM (Massachusetts, 1948): prospective cohort on cardiovascular risk factors; about 5,000 people aged 30-62 (original 6,000); 30-year study. RICHARD DOLL and BRADFORD HILL (1951): first cohort study linking smoking and lung cancer (smokers about 10 times more likely to die of it); Hill's nine criteria. Surgeon General's smoking report 1964. JONAS SALK: largest vaccine trial, inactivated polio vaccine (1950s); ALBERT SABIN: oral polio vaccine (1961). PAUL FARMER: co-founded Partners in Health, built hospital in Haiti after 2010 earthquake. PARACELSUS: father of toxicology. FREDERICK SANGER: dideoxy (Sanger) sequencing. ALICE STEWART: X-rays in pregnancy and childhood cancer (Oxford Survey); BRIAN MacMAHON: modern epidemiology text on study design; THEOBALD SMITH: hypothesis that pathogens evolve toward lower virulence (not always true: COVID spreads pre-symptomatically, fungi live in the environment). IBN AL-NAFIS: pulmonary circulation. Reed-Frost = chain-binomial model. Kermack and McKendrick (1927): SIR model. 1976 Yambuku Ebola: reused unsterilized needles, contact with body fluids and corpses; Legionnaires disease first recognized in Philadelphia 1976 (American Legion convention); Reston 1989 monkeys = Ebola Reston; Four Corners 1993 = hantavirus; NYC 1999 = West Nile; Omaha 1999 meatpackers = rubella; Wakefield 1998 MMR-autism paper: 12 children, no control group, fraud, retracted; vaccines do not cause autism. Black Death killed on the order of 75-200 million (Yersinia pestis, fleas, rodents). Texas sharpshooter fallacy = drawing the target around cases after the fact (cluster artifacts).

CHOLERA: Vibrio cholerae (Gram-negative bacterium); fecal-oral/water; rice-water diarrhea, treat with ORS (oral rehydration), CFR 1-2% (5.7% in Zimbabwe 2008 for lack of care); Snow. TUBERCULOSIS: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (acid-fast); airborne droplet nuclei; latent TB = no symptoms, normal chest X-ray, not contagious; TST read at 48-72 h; treat latent infection with isoniazid 6-9 months; notifiable. MALARIA: Plasmodium falciparum, vivax, ovale, malariae, knowlesi (protozoa); female Anopheles bite (biological vector); incubation 7-30 d (vivax/ovale hypnozoites sleep in liver); bed nets, standing-water control, prophylaxis; vaccine hard because the parasite hides and changes antigens. LYME: Borrelia burgdorferi (spirochete); Ixodes (black-legged) tick; reservoir white-footed mouse (deer are tick hosts but not competent; humans dead-end); nymphs cause most cases; erythema migrans rash 3-30 d; early tests may be negative; dilution effect (loss of biodiversity raises risk). PLAGUE: Yersinia pestis; flea bite (Xenopsylla), pneumonic by droplet. ANTHRAX: Bacillus anthracis (spore-forming Gram-positive); livestock/herbivores; cutaneous, inhalation, ingestion. BOTULISM: Clostridium botulinum toxin; canned food; infant botulism from honey; descending paralysis. TETANUS: Clostridium tetani, wounds, not person-to-person. LISTERIOSIS: Listeria monocytogenes; deli meats, soft cheese. LEGIONELLA: aerosolized water, cooling towers. TYPHOID: Salmonella Typhi; fecal-oral, carriers. SALMONELLOSIS: Salmonella (Enteritidis, Typhimurium, Newport); eggs, poultry, reptiles; incubation 6-72 h. E. COLI/STEC: Shiga toxin, HUS (hemolytic anemia, kidney failure, low platelets); incubation 1-10 d (mean 4). SHIGELLOSIS: fecal-oral, flies mechanical vector. CAMPYLOBACTER: C. jejuni, poultry, most common bacterial diarrhea cause. STAPH FOOD POISONING: S. aureus enterotoxin, onset 30 min-8 h (average 3 h), no fever. MRSA: methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, direct contact; tests call 50-70% of MRSA hospital-acquired (nosocomial). STREP: Streptococcus pyogenes; droplet; also scarlet and rheumatic fever. PERTUSSIS: Bordetella pertussis; droplet; waning acellular vaccine immunity. DIPHTHERIA: Corynebacterium. MENINGOCOCCAL: Neisseria meningitidis; droplet; vaccine; dorm outbreaks. PSITTACOSIS: Chlamydia psittaci from birds. RMSF: Rickettsia rickettsii (tick); EHRLICHIOSIS: tick. TULAREMIA: Francisella (ticks, rabbits). Q FEVER: Coxiella. GAS GANGRENE: Clostridium perfringens. FUNGI: candidiasis (Candida), aspergillosis, histoplasmosis (inhaled spores from bat/bird droppings; not bacterial). PROTOZOA: giardiasis (Giardia lamblia), cryptosporidiosis, cyclosporiasis, Chagas (Trypanosoma cruzi, kissing bug), sleeping sickness (tsetse). WORMS: trichinosis (Trichinella, undercooked pork or bear; fever, muscle pain, facial swelling, eosinophilia), tapeworm, hookworm, schistosomiasis (water contact). PRIONS (misfolded protein, no genetic material, not killed by heat alone, not alive, not a virus): scrapie, BSE (mad cow), kuru (ritual cannibalism, Papua New Guinea), CJD/Jakob-Creutzfeldt. VIRUSES (need host cells; antibiotics useless; capsid around DNA or RNA): influenza (RNA orthomyxovirus; drift/shift), measles (airborne; R0 12-18; rash 3-5 days after fever; 3 Cs cough, coryza, conjunctivitis; incubation 10-14 d), mumps, rubella, polio (picornavirus; fecal-oral; 1 in 200 infections paralyzed; Salk IPV, Sabin OPV; eradication in Afghanistan and Pakistan remaining), smallpox (variola; eradicated; R0 5), varicella (chickenpox; shingles = zoster reactivation, direct contact), hepatitis A (fecal-oral; picornavirus), hepatitis B (blood, sex), hepatitis C (blood), HIV (blood, sex, vertical; no cure; no vaccine), Ebola (Filoviridae; body fluids; bat reservoir; incubation 2-21 d), rabies (bite), rotavirus (fecal-oral), norovirus (Caliciviridae, above), dengue (Aedes aegypti/albopictus; 4 serotypes; no person-to-person; avoid aspirin), West Nile (Culex mosquito; birds reservoir), chikungunya (alphavirus, Aedes), Zika, yellow fever (Walter Reed), hantavirus (rodent excreta), SARS (coronavirus, bats, Hong Kong 2003), MERS (camels, South Korea 2015), COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2; R0 about 3; pre-symptomatic spread), mononucleosis (Epstein-Barr, saliva), herpes (HSV, no symptoms sometimes, sexual), HPV (genital warts, abnormal cells, cervical cancer), adenovirus (military vaccine types 4 and 7), reovirus (double-stranded RNA), rinderpest (virus, not prion). STIs: HIV (no cure); AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, caused by HIV); chlamydia (Chlamydia trachomatis); gonorrhea (Neisseria gonorrhoeae); syphilis (Treponema pallidum; sores, can cause blindness and death); herpes; HPV; PID (pelvic inflammatory disease: female infection of womb and ovaries from untreated STIs); trichomoniasis. Other old-notes diseases: Chicken pox (varicella zoster; droplet and direct), E. coli (fecal-oral), strep (droplet), tetanus (contact), athlete's foot (Tinea pedis, contact), Jakob-Creutzfeldt (prion, ingestion), tapeworm (nematode/cestode, ingestion), hepatitis A, B, C, Giardia (direct contact), influenza (droplet). Cystic fibrosis: two copies of the defective CFTR gene, most common mutation delta F508 (70%); Down syndrome: extra chromosome 21 (trisomy 21); oculocutaneous albinism genes TYR, OCA2, TYRP1, SLC45A2; hemophilia: blood does not clot, X-linked, more common in males.

MUTATIONS AND GENETICS TERMS. Mutations occur during cell division: substitution, deletion, insertion. Silent/synonymous (point) mutation = no change in amino acid; missense/nonsynonymous = one amino acid changed; nonsense = new stop codon. Antigenic drift (minor) and shift (major). SNP = single-nucleotide polymorphism; GWAS = genome-wide association study (case-control, Manhattan plot; marker SNPs near the causal gene by linkage disequilibrium). Penetrance = fraction with the genotype who show the trait; expressivity = degree of expression.

LAB AND DATA TERMS. ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) detects antibodies or antigens; rapid antigen tests show CURRENT infection, antibody tests show past exposure and miss early infection; RT-PCR/PCR amplifies genetic material (gold standard for norovirus, COVID); stool culture confirms bacterial foodborne illness; Gram stain: thick peptidoglycan retains crystal violet = Gram-positive (purple), thin = Gram-negative (pink); bacteria are prokaryotes (no nucleus), bacilli are rods, cocci spheres, spirochetes spirals; endospores survive harsh conditions; antibiotics do not work on viruses; bacteriostatic stops growth, bactericidal kills; endotoxin = lipopolysaccharide of Gram-negative cell walls, exotoxin = secreted. Biosafety levels: BSL-1 harmless E. coli, BSL-2 Salmonella, S. aureus, HIV, BSL-3 TB, West Nile, SARS-CoV, BSL-4 Ebola, Marburg, Lassa (smallpox stocks at CDC Atlanta and a Russian lab). ROC curve: a curve near the top-left corner has area (AUC) near 1 = accurate; AUC of a perfect test = 1.0. FIGO cancer stages: I confined to organ of origin, II invasion of nearby organs or tissue, III spread to distant nodes or tissue within the pelvis, IV distant metastasis. Scientific method: observe, question, research, hypothesize, experiment, test hypothesis, conclude, report. Clinical interview collects behaviors, attitudes, situation, personality, life history. Meta-analysis = pools results of independent studies. PulseNet concept: analyze bacterial DNA to find where else the strain is. Symptom vs sign; Quarantine vs isolation (above). Sufficient cause / necessary cause (Rothman). Notifiable vs reportable. U=U. Health Institutions: CDC protects America from health, safety and security threats, conducts experiments, informs the public, responds to crises (part of the US Department of Health and Human Services, Atlanta); WHO (Geneva); APHA (American Public Health Association); NIOSH (occupational safety and health); FDA, USDA/FSIS, HHS.

POLICY AND OTHER. Disease control policy steps: problem identification, policy analysis, strategy and policy development, policy enactment. Public health policy = laws, regulations, actions and decisions that promote wellness and meet health goals. Top public health concerns: obesity, cancer, diabetes, mental health, climate change, equity. Health disparity = preventable differences in disease burden among groups. WHO definition of health: complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely absence of disease. Hygiene hypothesis: early microbial exposure (farm children) lowers asthma and allergy. Cluster investigation caution: counts are not rates (compare New Mexico and Pennsylvania with rates per 100,000 and age adjustment). Healthy-user bias: HRT users seemed to have less heart disease, later disproved by RCTs. TB contact investigation: concentric circles by risk, TST repeated 8-10 weeks after exposure. Contact tracing and exclusion of sick food handlers 48 h.

LIKELY TEST FACTS TO KNOW COLD. Measures: attack rate (outbreak), RR (cohort), OR (case-control), prevalence (cross-sectional), incidence (cohort). Gold standard of evidence for causation: RCT. Only essential Hill criterion: temporality. Study with no control group: case series. Hospital controls: Berkson bias; remembering exposures differently: recall bias; earlier detection looks like longer survival: lead-time bias. Clinical vs public health: individual vs population. Outbreak vs epidemic vs pandemic vs endemic vs sporadic vs cluster (above). Passive vs active vs sentinel vs syndromic surveillance (above). Primary prevention = before disease, secondary = screening, tertiary = limit disability, primordial = stop risk factors, quaternary = avoid over-medicalization. Point source curve = single peak; propagated = growing waves. First steps of an investigation: prepare, establish it is an outbreak, verify the diagnosis. Smallpox: only eradicated human disease. Polio: remaining in Pakistan and Afghanistan. Typhoid Mary: asymptomatic carrier. Koch postulates need pure culture. DALY = YLL + YLD. HIT = 1 - 1/R0.

MORE MATH RULES FOUND BY PRACTICE TESTS. SERIES testing (both must be positive): overall sensitivity = Se1 x Se2 and overall specificity = 1 - (1-Sp1)(1-Sp2); PARALLEL (either positive): sensitivity = 1 - (1-Se1)(1-Se2), specificity = Sp1 x Sp2; the second test's PPV/NPV uses only the people referred on (first-test positives) as its N. Specificity = TN/(TN+FP) where the denominator is everyone truly non-diseased by the gold standard. Subgroup risk factors (rates by age, sex, deck, emote, food): compare each group's rate with the reference group (RR = group rate/reference rate); RR > 1 risk factor, the larger the stronger; check each subgroup (sex pattern too). OR approximates RR when the disease is rare, cases represent all cases and controls represent all non-diseased. To cut Re from 1.5 to 1 with R0 = 12: susceptible fraction falls from 1.5/12 = 12.5% to 1/12 = 8.33%, so an extra 4.17% of the population must be immune; R0 = contacts x P(transmission) (24 contacts, R0 12 gives P = 0.5). Earlier screening with no cure: true incidence unchanged but diagnosed time (lead time) longer, so prevalence and apparent survival rise, mortality unchanged (lead-time bias) - not an incidence rise. If a stem's table totals do not match its text (patients, sick counts), compute from the cell values and state the discrepancy. "Less than 5% probability the result is due to chance" is the usual accepted p < 0.05 wording. Select-all items: only tick options directly consistent with the definition (P = I x D: lower incidence cannot give higher prevalence unless duration rises).

DESIGN, PREVENTION AND CONTROL RULES. Outbreak with a known guest list = retrospective cohort (attack rates, RR) even if the table lists ill vs well; case-control only if controls were sampled separately (OR); risk factors for an outcome that already happened = case-control. Cohort aliases: longitudinal, prospective, follow-up, incidence study. Nested case-control: controls sampled from cohort members still at risk when each case occurs; case-cohort: a random subcohort picked at baseline serves many outcomes; both are cheaper, measure exposure before disease (less recall bias), and suit rare outcomes. Case-crossover: each case is its own control (hazard window vs earlier window) for transient exposures and acute outcomes. Ecological time trend = historical trends in population rates; a single-arm uncontrolled intervention = clinical trial; a "cohort" where only cases are interviewed = case series. Case-control = retrospective only; cohort = prospective or retrospective. Elimination of an existing hazard or banning a supplier = primary prevention; primordial = prevent the risk factor from arising (policy, built environment); naloxone after overdose = tertiary; screening such as blood pressure checks = secondary. Control strategies: source removal/reduction (eliminate the reservoir or contaminated source: recall food, confiscate drug supply, remediate soil or water), host protection (vaccine, prophylaxis), break transmission (hygiene, masks, distancing), isolation (sick) and quarantine (exposed), surveillance and education; environmental remediation suits non-infectious hazards; a non-infectious outbreak = toxin or non-biological agent, not person-to-person. Natural history: PREPATHOGENESIS = before disease starts in the host (susceptible host plus risk factors present), ending when the agent first acts on the host; "point of volition" = the point at which the person decides to seek care. CARRIER stems that give no timing: incubatory if before symptoms, convalescent if after, chronic if long-term (Typhoid Mary); some tests use "passive" for any healthy carrier. Sentinel surveillance = designated sites report regularly (a type of enhanced/active). Outbreak keys sometimes list "find cases" and "describe person, place, time" as separate steps; if exactly 10 steps are asked use the CDC list. Detection bias = testing only symptomatic or severe cases misses mild ones. HAI (hospital infections) rise from more susceptible patients, invasive devices and procedures, and antimicrobial-resistant organisms; TB rises with drug resistance (MDR/XDR), HIV, crowding and poverty, migration, reduced screening, waning BCG. Hill examples: disease only in the exposed = specificity; larger RR = less likely wholly due to bias or confounding; top three usually cited: temporality, strength, consistency or dose-response.

BODY-SYSTEM BIOLOGY SEEN ON TESTS. GUT: the large intestine holds most microbes (10^11-10^12 per gram); Peyer's patches = lymphoid follicles in the small intestine (ileum); M (microfold) cells carry antigens from the gut lumen to Peyer's patches. RESPIRATORY: upper tract has normal flora (colonization resistance: commensals compete for nutrients and attachment sites and make bacteriocins); the lower tract is kept near sterile by the mucociliary escalator, cough, alveolar macrophages and secretory IgA; TB is inhaled in low doses to alveolar macrophages and T cells (granuloma). LIPIDS: cholesterol travels in lipoproteins; LDL = "bad", HDL carries cholesterol back; macrophages engulf oxidized LDL to become foam cells, then fatty streak and plaque (atherosclerosis). VIRAL GENETICS: influenza = segmented negative-sense ssRNA whose polymerase lacks proofreading (frequent mutation = drift; segment reassortment = shift); (-)ssRNA must make mRNA with its own RNA polymerase, (+)ssRNA is itself mRNA, retroviruses go RNA to DNA to mRNA, dsDNA viruses transcribe mRNA from DNA.
