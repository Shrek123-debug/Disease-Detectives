[STIMULUS] Beyond ecological impact, environmental contaminants have important effects on human health. One way to measure these effects is through chronic daily intake. Simplified Bioavailability Extraction Test (SBET) was used to measure the bioaccessibility of contaminants. The chronic daily intake (CDI) is defined similarly to average daily intake (ADI) referenced earlier. Again, the calculation yields a value that gives the amount of a contaminant ingested over the body weight of an individual per unit time. For each metal, the potential health risk can then be calculated as a Hazard Quotient (HQ), which was mentioned earlier, or a Cancer Risk (CR).

Essentially, these two measures are finding the overall health risk (or cancer risk) for each contaminant and standardizing based on some reference dose relative to the chronic daily intake. Oral reference dose (RfD₀) and cancer slope factor (CSF₀) are found from screening levels, relative bioavailability (RBA) is the ratio of SBET extraction to total contents for each contaminant.

=== Part III: Salmonellosis ===

[STIMULUS] Salmonella enterica is a gram-negative bacteria that often infects livestock, primarily cattle and poultry. S. enterica can cause illness in humans and is the primary bacterial cause of foodborne illness in the United States, causing an estimated 1 million cases and 400 deaths every year. Salmonella patients typically exhibit symptoms consistent with acute gastroenteritis such as diarrhea, fever, and stomach cramps. Symptoms may begin as soon as six hours after exposure and usually last for four to seven days.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) coordinates PulseNet, a national surveillance network for DNA fingerprints of bacterial agents. DNA fingerprinting allows for the formation of clusters of disease caused by similar strains of a bacteria. Typically, salmonellosis outbreaks within the United States are identified through PulseNet.
