[STIMULUS] It was a scorching July in 2000, and the Gulf of Taranto, a picturesque corner of southern Italy, was alive with the usual summer buzz: sunburned tourists jostling for gelato, children squealing by the sea, and locals debating whether it was too hot even for espresso. But beneath the idyllic surface, trouble was brewing—not in the espresso machines, but in the stomachs of the resort-goers. A total of 456 guest rooms in 19 buildings, equipped with a restaurant, swimming pool, and resort management office.

Through the 7th-31st, what began as a few whispered complaints of queasy bellies quickly spiraled into a full-blown outbreak, reporting diarrhea or vomiting. Sun-kissed travelers were now pale and wilted, clutching their stomachs. The local medical clinic was overrun, its waiting room packed with a mix of concerned tourists and grumbling locals wondering who had cursed their summer.

Rumors began to swirl: Was it the seafood? The tap water? That suspiciously warm tiramisu at the buffet? As the theories grew wilder—alien microbes in the sea, a secret pasta conspiracy—it became clear that an expert was needed to cut through the chaos.

Enter you, the amateur epidemiologist. With your crisp white lab coat (recently upgraded to include extra pockets for snacks) and a field kit at the ready, you were there first-hand.

The resort’s manager, a man whose tan had faded dramatically over the past week, approached you with a desperate plea. "We need answers, doctor," he said, his sunglasses slipping down his nose. "And fast. These tourists didn’t pay for this kind of thrill."
