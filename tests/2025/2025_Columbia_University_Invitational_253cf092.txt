[STIMULUS] **Case Study #1**

In 1981, an outbreak of a bacterial illness called Salmonella muenchen began in Steubenville, Ohio. Over the course of a few months (starting in January), there were 36 cases of diarrhea, abdominal pain, and fever reported. The cases ranged from a one-month-old baby to a 73-year-old woman. The outbreak even spread to Michigan, and the source of the illness was still unknown.

Two investigators, Dr. Taylor and Emmet Schmidt, were handed the mysterious case. They interviewed affected families to look for a common source due to the nature of salmonella, but none of the households reported anything unusual about their diets. The only clue that the investigators had was that many of the reported cases were young adults aged 20-29, or infected families had someone in that age range living in their home.

29c. [frq, 2 pt] What does the age range (infected households all included someone aged 20-29) possibly indicate about the source of the outbreak?

29d. [frq, 3 pt] Taylor and Schmidt eventually asked the sick families about drug use. After some initial hesitancy, one family admitted that they and their friends occasionally smoked marijuana. They shared a sample with the investigators, and lab results confirmed that the weed was contaminated with salmonella. Because of the time period and location, drug use was highly stigmatized and illegal. How would you balance people's worries about getting into legal trouble with your responsibilities as an epidemiologist?

[STIMULUS] **Case Study #3**

Over 65% of children 5 years-of-age and under in the United States require out-of-home child care. Childcare attendance has been associated with an elevated risk of respiratory illness and acute gastroenteritis. While child care associated respiratory disease cases are more numerous, acute gastroenteritis (AGE) typically causes more severe symptoms and more than double the number of absences from child care. In addition, viral pathogens such as norovirus, rotavirus, and adenovirus are highly infectious and may be spread to parents and other household members. Fomites – inanimate objects that may harbor contamination – are often thought to facilitate the spread of viral AGE pathogens, as symptomatic children may shed substantial amounts of virus onto objects in their environment through episodes of diarrhea and vomiting.

**Data Analysis and Interpretation**

Transmission models are often used to simulate outbreaks and plan interventions. A transmission model was constructed to represent the spread of acute gastroenteritis pathogens in child care and primary school settings. In contrast to real outbreaks, model outbreaks can be simulated many times to examine how an outbreak might have progressed under different conditions. In this model, hygiene and sanitation interventions were tested to evaluate their efficacy. Each simulation was started with 1 sick child and 24 well children. 10,000 total simulations were conducted for each intervention tested. Simulations represent a total time of 1 month.
