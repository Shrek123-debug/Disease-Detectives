[STIMULUS] Though James Lind observed and treated cases of malaria, he did not produce a theory of malaria that stood the test of time. In 1906 Camilio Golgi won the Nobel prize for the discovery of the cause of the disease. Golgi’s critical observation: malaria is caused by cell rupture and release of merozoites. Use the passages below (taken from International Journal of Mosquito Research 2020; 7(2): 08-10 and the CDC’s Clinical Features of Malaria) to answer questions about the natural history, spectrum of disease, and chain of infection in malaria.

**International Journal of Mosquito Research 2020; 7(2): 08-10**

The life cycle of Plasmodium is very complicated and begins with an infective stage, where sporozoites are injected into humans by Anopheles and ultimately invade hepatocytes where they undergo exoerythrocytic schizogony (first phase of asexual reproduction) and produce merozoites. These merozoites enter blood and undergo erythrocytic schizogony (second phase of asexual reproduction) resulting in the production of more merozoites.

This process is repeated indefinitely which causes malaria. After this young merozoites develop into male and female gametocytes and are taken up by female Anopheles. In Anopheles these gametocytes mature into male and female gametes and fertilize and produce a motile zygote called ookinete (sporogony). Ookinete penetrates the gut wall and becomes oocyst that further produce sporozoites that migrate to salivary glands of Anopheles and are injected to humans, when the Anopheles bites them. The first evidence of malaria parasite was found in mosquitoes preserved in amber from the Cenozoic era that are approximately 30 million years old.

**Clinical Features of Malaria**

Following the infective bite by the female Anopheles mosquito, a period of time (the "incubation period") goes by before the first symptoms appear in a patient. The incubation period in most cases varies from 7 to 30 days. The shorter periods are most frequently observed with Plasmodium falciparum and the longer ones with Plasmodium malariae.

Antimalarial drugs taken for prophylaxis by travelers are highly effective if taken as prescribed. However, antimalarial prophylaxis occasionally can delay the appearance of malaria symptoms by weeks or months, long after the traveler has left the malaria-endemic area. (This can happen particularly with Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale, both of which can produce dormant liver stage parasites; the liver stages may reactivate and cause disease months after the infective mosquito bite.)

Progression to severe malaria occurs when infections are complicated by serious organ failures or abnormalities in the patient's blood or metabolism, usually following delays in diagnosis and treatment.
