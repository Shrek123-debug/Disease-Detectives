[STIMULUS] In epidemiology, the SIR model can be used to model the spread of a disease under appropriate conditions. S, I, and R are variables that stand for susceptible, infected, and recovered/removed. These variables represent people at a specific stage of disease and can be made into functions of time (S(t), I(t), and R(t)). Note that people can move from these variables as a disease progresses. N, or the total population, is equal to S(t) + I(t) + R(t). The rate of change, or derivative, of each of these functions can be derived to model the change in S, I, and R (which, in turn, models the spread of disease). dS/dt is inversely proportional to S*I, as the change in susceptible population is dependent on the probability of susceptible and infected populations coming into contact (dS/dt = -βSI, where β is a constant). dI/dt is equal to the inverse of dS/dt minus the proportion of infected people who become recovered/removed (dI/dt = βSI - γI, where β and γ is a constant). Finally, dR/dt is equal to the inverse of dI/dt (dR/dt = γI, where γ is a constant). Combined, these three equations make up a nonlinear system of differential equations that is capable of modeling the spread of many infectious diseases.¹

1 Note that the SIR model rests on four assumptions: 1. There is a constant, closed population (N), 2. There is a constant rate of transmission and removal, 3. There are no births or deaths, and 4. A well mixed population.
