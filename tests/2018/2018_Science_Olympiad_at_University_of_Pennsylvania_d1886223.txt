=== PART I: GI illness in Seattle ===

[STIMULUS] It’s a Monday morning, and you’re sitting in your office at the CDC reviewing paperwork when your colleague, Dr. Sims, knocks on your door.

“Hey, ya got a minute? Think we might have a foodborne outbreak on our hands up in the Pacific Northwest. Problem is, no one’s been able to figure out what’s causing it.”

“Sure,” you respond, “Come on in. What’s goin’ on?”

1. [frq, 2 pt] Define what an outbreak is.

2. [frq, 4 pt] List four reasons why you might investigate an outbreak of foodborne illness.

[STIMULUS] “Well,” Dr. Sims continues, “there have been a lot of unidentified cases of gastrointestinal illness cropping up at hospitals in and around Seattle. Hospitals are reporting patients coming in with watery stools, nausea, and vomiting. Some patients, though not all, are also feverish. No one has died so far, but a few have had to be hospitalized for dehydration. The hospitals reached out to the Seattle & King County Department of Public Health, who contacted us immediately.”

3. [frq, 2 pt] Given the definition you wrote and the information above, explain whether or not this scenario currently constitutes an outbreak.

[STIMULUS] “One last thing to add,” she continues, “Not every gastrointestinal illness is able to be identified at the hospitals, so some cases always remain a mystery. The hospitals report seeing a total of 73 cases of this mystery illness during the past 4 months alone, starting on May 17th and ending September 24th. The expected rate of mystery gastrointestinal illnesses that Seattle sees is 24 per 100,000 per year, and the population of Seattle is a little over 600,000.”

4a. [frq, 3 pt] Assuming that the rate for the past 4 months was constant throughout the entire year, calculate the incidence rate of the mystery illness and compare it to the expected rate.

4b. [frq, 6 pt] Using an appropriate statistical hypothesis testing procedure, demonstrate whether this number of cases is significantly higher than the expected.
    
    HINT 1: Index n = 600,000, parameter π = 24/100,000.
    HINT 2: Do not attempt to calculate a p-value. Instead, define a critical region in which the result must fall in order for you to reject your null hypothesis. Use a cutoff value of 1.96, which corresponds to α = 0.05 for a one-sided test.
    HINT 3: Use a continuity correction of ½ stepsize.
