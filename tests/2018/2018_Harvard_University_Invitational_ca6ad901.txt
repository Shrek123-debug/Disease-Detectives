[STIMULUS] Campylobacter and Salmonella are leading causes of bacterial gastroenteritis in the United States and are estimated to cause more than 1 million episodes of domestically acquired illness annually. Campylobacter and Salmonella are primarily transmitted through contaminated food, but animal-to-human and human-to-human transmission can also occur. Most people who become ill with campylobacteriosis get diarrhea, cramping, abdominal pain, and fever within two to five days after exposure to the organism. In people with compromised immune systems, Campylobacter occasionally spreads to the bloodstream and causes a serious life-threatening condition. Approximately 76 people with Campylobacter infections die each year. The most common species of the genus is Campylobacter jejuni, which are spiral-shaped gram-negative bacteria which grow optimally at 37 to 42 °C. This range of incubation temperatures is ideally suited for growth in birds, which have a body temperature of approximately 41 to 42 °C. Most people with salmonellosis develop diarrhea, fever, and abdominal cramps between 12 and 72 hours after infection. Salmonellosis can be fatal when infection spreads from the intestines to the bloodstream. Approximately 450 people die each year from salmonellosis in the United States. Salmonella are gram-negative, rod-shaped bacteria that can be divided into two species: S. enterica and S. bongori. There are more than 30 different serotypes of Salmonella; the most common serotypes are Salmonella Typherimurium, Enteriditis, Newport, and Javiana.

[STIMULUS] Botulinum toxin is the most acutely lethal toxin known, with an estimated median lethal dose (LD₅₀) of 1.3-2.1 ng/kg. (For comparison, the lethal dose for potassium cyanide is 5,000,000-10,000,000 ng/kg, or 5-10 mg/kg.) Botulinum toxin inhibits the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh) from neurons. ACh is critical for motor neuron function, as it signals for muscles to contract in response to electrical impulses. Botulinum toxin thus leads to flaccid paralysis and sometimes death, as the diaphragm ceases to contract. The toxin is synthesized by the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, the bacteria that causes foodborne botulism.
