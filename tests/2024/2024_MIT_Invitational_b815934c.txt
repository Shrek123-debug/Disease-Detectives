=== Part 1. Flu Season ===

[STIMULUS] After getting your annual flu shot, you decide to do a little reading about this disease. The most well-known flu pandemic is probably the 1918 Spanish Flu pandemic (which likely didn't start in Spain, it just didn't censor news about the disease while other countries, which were in WW1, did).

The following is an excerpt about the 1918 flu pandemic from the following paper:

Lagacé-Wiens, Philippe R. S. MD, DTM&H, FRCPC; Rubinstein, Ethan MD, LLB; Gumel, Abba PhD. Influenza epidemiology—past, present, and future.

"...the attack rates and age-specific incidence rates of this pandemic did not differ significantly from subsequent pandemics, this pandemic was characterized by its particularly high rates of morbidity and mortality [in] young adults … The reason for the high mortality rates remain unclear, although recent analysis of genetic reconstruction of the 1918 strain suggest that the virus itself was more virulent and had a propensity to cause viral pneumonia, as well as a clinical picture compatible with a "cytokine storm". Controversy remains regarding whether the virus itself, or its strong association with postviral bacterial pneumonia, was the major cause of death in 1918. The overall evidence today supports that both mechanisms, direct viral virulence and postviral superinfection, played major roles in causing the high mortality."

[STIMULUS] Source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7316090/

The most basic model of disease spread is SIR (susceptible, infected, recovered/removed), where people move between these three groups with no chance of re-infections. However, for influenza, we also need to account for people in the latent (L) stage and asymptomatic individuals (A) who never get sick, but can still spread flu before they recover. However, we can model that asymptomatic people have a reduced infectivity (by a factor of δ). Now, I only refers to symptomatic infected individuals.
