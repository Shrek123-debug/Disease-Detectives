=== Part I: abstracted from Upson K, De Roos AJ, Thompson ML, Sathyanarayana S, Scholes D, Barr DB, Holt VL. 2013. Organochlorine pesticides and risk of endometriosis: findings from a population-based case–control study. Environ Health Perspect 121:1319–1324; http://dx.doi.org/10.1289/ehp.1306648 ===

[STIMULUS] Endometriosis is associated with substantial morbidity in women, including chronic pain, bleeding and infertility. While its etiology is not fully understood, it is considered to be an estrogen-dependent disease. This suggests that environmental chemicals which mimic or alter estrogen activity may plausibly affect endometriosis risk. Organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) have exhibited estrogenic properties in animal and in vitro studies. While their use in the U.S. has been banned for some decades, they are still used in many other countries. Furthermore, they are fat-soluble, highly mobile and break down very slowly in the body. Presumably for these reasons, much of the US population has detectable serum concentrations of many OCPs.

The National Institute of Child Health and Human Development funded a large study of the possible factors associated with between levels of select OCPs and endometriosis, which was analyzed by a group of researchers based at the University of Washington. 340 women aged 19-49 who were enrolled in a large health care system in Washington State and who had received a first-time diagnosis of endometriosis between 1 April 1996 and 31 March 2001 were invited to participate, as were 741 randomly selected other women in the same age group who were enrolled in the same health system and who had never been diagnosed with endometriosis. 89.7% of endometriosis patients and 85.1% of other members accepted the invitation. After exclusions, blood samples were collected from 283 patients and 585 other members. Blood lipids were measured, and serum levels of several OCPs for each participant were determined by gas chromatography – mass spectrometry. The median level of each OCP was calculated for the group of subjects who did not have an endometriosis diagnosis. Table 1 records the number of individuals in each group who were above and below the calculated median value; totals are not the same for each group because in many cases, the presence of another substance in the blood interfered with the ability to measure pesticide concentration. The median value of the pesticide Mirex was below the limit of the instrument to detect accurately, so any value above the limit of detection (LOD) was reported.

=== Part II: abstracted from N Engl J Med. 2002 Sep 19;347(12):869-77. Environmental exposure to endotoxin and its relation to asthma in school-age children. Braun-Fahrländer C, Riedler J, Herz U, Eder W, Waser M, Grize L, Maisch S, Carr D, Gerlach F, Bufe A, Lauener RP, Schierl R, Renz H, Nowak D, von Mutius E; Allergy and Endotoxin Study Team. ===

[STIMULUS] Researchers from the Institute of Social and Preventative Medicine in Basel, Switzerland surveyed thousands of parents in defined rural areas of Switzerland, Germany and Austria for health, demographic and occupational information and asked for permission to sample of their children’s blood and dust from their mattresses. 812 children from farming and non-farm households with complete data and similar demographics were selected for the study, which was designed to investigate the link between exposure to bacteria during childhood and the development of allergic responses, especially atopic conditions such as asthma. Dust samples were analyzed for the presence of dust and storage mites and cat dander and also for bacterial endotoxin, which persists after the death of the bacteria which produced it. Blood samples were analyzed for the presence of IgE antibodies to common allergens, such as dust and storage mites, grass and birch pollen, and cat and cow dander. Tables 2 and 3 record some of their findings.
