24. [frq, 8 pt] Consider a hypothetical diagnostic test for rare condition X that has **95% sensitivity and specificity**. Let's also say that condition X has **1% prevalence** in the general population. Let A be the event that a person has the disease, let A^C ("A complement") be the event that a person does not have the disease, and let B be the event that a person tests positive with our test. We want to find what the positive predictive value (PPV) of our test is; written in math terms, we want to find P(A|B)—the probability of having the disease given a positive test (we use A|B to denote the event A, given the event B. So, for example, the sensitivity of our test would be P(B|A); it is the probability of testing positive given you have the disease).
    
    A famous formula called Bayes' Theorem gives us the following:
    
    P(A|B) = P(B|A)*P(A)/P(B) = P(B|A)*P(A) / ((P(B|A)*P(A))+(P(B|A^c)*P(A^c)))
    
    Using this formula, calculate the PPV of our hypothetical test. (This formula is actually where the formula you probably have in your notes for PPV comes from!). Express your answer as a percentage and round to 1 decimal place.

=== Part 3 - Lyme Time ===

[STIMULUS] In 1975, physicians in the small town of Lyme, Connecticut noticed an unusual pattern of illness. Young patients presented with a range of symptoms, including erythema migrans (circular rashes) and manifestations resembling rheumatoid arthritis. A common feature among these patients was a reported tick bite approximately one week prior to symptom onset. The illness was subsequently named Lyme Disease after the town in which it was first described. It wasn't until 1981 that the causative agent of Lyme Disease – a spirochete bacteria called Borrelia burgdorferi – was isolated and linked to the disease. Since its initial description in Lyme, Connecticut, the disease has spread rapidly throughout the United States, particularly in the Midwest and along the east coast.

Borrelia burgdorferi inhabits the hindgut of the deer tick, Ixodes scapularis, where it does not cause disease in the tick. Ixodes scapularis feeds on host blood, and during this process transmission of B. burgdorferi can occur bidirectionally: ticks may acquire the bacterium from infected hosts, and hosts may become infected following a tick bite. Humans are considered dead-end hosts, as ticks do not acquire B. burgdorferi when feeding on infected humans. The tick undergoes three life stages, each requiring a single blood meal: the larval stage, during which it typically feeds on small mammals such as the white-footed mouse; the nymph stage, during which it feeds on larger mammals including deer and humans; and the adult stage, in which it again feeds on larger mammals. The majority of human Lyme disease cases result from bites by nymphal ticks, which have already taken a blood meal and are sufficiently small to evade detection.
