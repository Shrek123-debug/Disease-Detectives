[STIMULUS] The following case study is completely made up, any resemblance to real life figures or events is coincidental.

Dr. Imogen Vale, a senior field epidemiologist at the Department of Environmental Health, was called to investigate an unusual rise in chronic fatigue and skin irritation among urban gardeners in Brookmere City. The condition, later termed Blight Fever, presented with mild fevers, weakness, and a faintly luminescent rash visible under UV light. Concern escalated when local physicians noticed a pattern: nearly all affected gardeners worked in one of three community greenhouses that had recently switched to a new soil additive called EcoBloom X, designed to improve soil nitrogen retention.

To determine whether EcoBloom X was linked to Blight Fever, Dr. Vale and her team identified all active gardeners in the three greenhouses and recorded whether they had developed Blight Fever within three months of EcoBloom X’s introduction. They also measured each gardener’s level of exposure to the additive, categorizing them as high, medium, or low exposure based on hours spent handling treated soil. A blood test was later developed to detect a biomarker called BF-α, suspected to be associated with the illness.

Dr. Vale’s team followed gardeners, observing who developed Blight Fever over time while documenting their baseline exposures and environmental factors such as greenhouse ventilation and humidity. Participants were contacted biweekly for symptom monitoring and lab testing. While this design provided valuable incidence data, the project required significant logistical coordination and funding, and many participants dropped out midway, citing time constraints and fatigue.
